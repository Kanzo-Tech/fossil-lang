title: Identity
description: Why the subject's identity is a slot the language owns rather than an attribute argument, and why every mapping that produces a type has to mint the same one.
---

Every node in an RDF graph is an IRI, and an IRI is not in your CSV. Somebody has to mint it. Where
that instruction lives is the hardest question in the language's surface, and it is the one place
where both documents a program is written against — the source and the shape — are silent.

## The shape document cannot declare it

A shape document describes the predicates of a node. In RDF the subject **is** the node, not one of
its properties, so there is no slot in the document to assign an identity to. That is not an
oversight in ShEx or in SHACL, and choosing the other one does not fix it.

The comparison that makes it clear is with the schema languages that *do* declare identity —
`identifier: true` in LinkML, `@id` in Prisma, `@key` in GraphQL. All three describe property graphs,
and in a property graph identity is **signalled**: one of the fields you already have is the one that
identifies. In RDF identity is **constructed**, out of a row, as an IRI that exists nowhere in the
source.

So the instruction lives in the program, and the question is what shape it takes there.

## Not an attribute argument

The attractive form is an attribute — `@subject(iri = "…/{User.email}")` — because an attribute is
where metadata goes and this is metadata. It does not survive contact with the prior art: seven
languages, no exception, none of which admits a per-instance expression in an attribute argument, and
one of which calls the restriction a deliberate device in its own specification. The seven and the
control experiment are the [identity table in prior art](/docs/design/prior-art#identity); the
alternative and what would bring it back are [discarded](/docs/design/discarded).

The prior art also prescribes the exit, and it is never *widen the attribute slot*: give the thing
its own named construct, as SQL does with `GENERATED ALWAYS AS` and Django with `GeneratedField`. The
rule behind that is sharp enough to apply mechanically — an attribute that can be omitted implies a
derived fallback, and a derived identity is one nobody wrote down; an attribute that cannot be
omitted is syntax with brackets, and then the spelling does not matter.

## The slot, and why `@`

fossil takes the prescribed exit. Identity is a slot the language owns, written as an assignment on
the first line of a mapping body:

<Program src="shop/shop.fossil" region="identity" title="shop.fossil" />

Its right-hand side is the ordinary expression grammar — the same expressions, the same checking, the
same completion. There is no second mini-language to keep in sync with the first, which is the whole
reason the string-in-an-attribute form is dangerous.

The `@` marks the language's own namespace against the bare names a shape document declares. Property
keys are the last segment of a predicate IRI, and a vocabulary is free to declare a predicate called
`subject`; without a reserved marker the language and the user's vocabulary compete for the same
word. JSON-LD reserves `@`-prefixed keywords for exactly this reason. The mechanism generalises: a
second language-owned slot, the named graph a triple belongs to for instance, enters through the same
door instead of opening another.

(`#` was the other candidate and is disqualified three times over: it is the comment character in
both Turtle and SPARQL, it is the fragment separator in half the RDF vocabularies in existence, and a
bare `#` is not lexable in this grammar at all.)

## Two obligations no other property has

Because an IRI *denotes*, the identity slot carries two rules no ordinary property carries.

**It is deterministic and bounded to the row.** An identity that depends on anything that is not the
data is not reproducible, and a corpus you cannot regenerate is not a corpus. This is a check, not a
style guide.

**Every mapping that produces a type writes the same one.** Two mappings producing `Person` with
different identity templates is an error, and the diagnostic names both mappings and both templates:

<Program src="errors/two-identities/expected/diagnostic.txt" title="fossil check program.fossil" />

Error rather than warning, because of what happens when the warning is ignored: two entities in the
graph where there was one, discovered by whoever queries it. A warning is an appropriate response to
something the user might have meant. Nobody means this.

Nothing else in the mapping world checks it, and the one system that shipped the check removed it —
both of those are answered in [discarded](/docs/design/discarded), where the reversal conditions live
too. The short version is that the removal is correct there for a reason that argues *for* the check
here: those subgraphs deploy separately and nobody ever holds the whole set, while fossil compiles a
whole program at once.

## The cost

Two sources describing **disjoint populations of the same type with different identity schemes** stop
being expressible. A legacy export keyed by account number and a current export keyed by email cannot
both produce `Person` in one program without agreeing on how a `Person` is named. That is a real
restriction, accepted knowingly.

One piece of Linked Data guidance plays against the design and should be said rather than omitted:
its unit of minting policy is the **publisher**, not the type. fossil chooses the type because the
compiler is the single authority over a program, which is an argument from topology rather than from
that literature.
