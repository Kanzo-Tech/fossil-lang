---
title: Where x and y come from
description: "A vertex table is drawn at two of its columns, and `fossil.json` says which and who chose them: the writer's joint layout over the whole graph, which is the default, or the program's own columns. A table with no position is not drawn."
---

Zoom far enough out of a million-vertex graph and the individual vertices stop being visible. What is
left is a distribution — dense here, empty there — and whether it means anything depends on one fact:
whether the coordinates were measured outside the renderer, or chosen by an algorithm because a
picture needed coordinates. Both are two numeric columns called `x` and `y`, so the manifest is the
only place the difference can be said.

## Two modes, and the manifest names the one a table was written in

Every vertex table in `fossil.json` has a `position` or has none —
`crates/fossil-sinks/src/manifest.rs, Position`:

| `position` | who chose the columns | the columns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `{ "by": "layout", "x": "x", "y": "y" }` | the writer's layout pass | `x`, `y` as `float`, with `cluster_id` beside them |
| `{ "by": "program", "x": "lon", "y": "lat" }` | the program, which declared two of its own columns | whatever the program named |
| absent | nobody | the table is **not drawn** |

**No absent table is drawn, and none is placed at a default.** A reader that invented a position for
it would draw the invention, which is the one thing this page exists to prevent. Its vertices still
have ids — the last ones, ordered by type and then by subject — and it is still read, filtered and
joined like any other table.

**What would reverse it.** A reader that has to draw every type and has no position for some. Then
that reader places them, in the view, and the corpus still says it did not.

## `layout` is one pass over the whole graph

The default is **one** layout over the union of every vertex type, with every relation in it — the
ones between types included. Louvain over that graph gives a hierarchy of communities;
`cluster_id` is its cut; `crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/place.rs, cluster_layout` places the
communities and packs each one's members; and the position's Hilbert rank over the whole plane is the
`dense_id` — `crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/hilbert.rs, hilbert_codes` quantised with `floor`, so
that the order is exactly the one DuckDB spatial's `ST_Hilbert` computes over the same extent. That
agreement is measured, not assumed: flooring agreed with `ST_Hilbert` on **2,000 of 2,000** points,
and the rounding it replaced on **504**. A reader can therefore recompute the order in SQL, and none
has to: every table is written in it.

There are no per-type planes. The writer used to lay each type out on its own and set the planes side
by side, which drew a multi-type graph as islands joined by long lines — the relations between types
were exactly the edges the placement never saw. [The derived plane](/docs/design/later/position-plane)
keeps what the per-type placement measured.

**It is deterministic, and that was measured rather than argued**: two processes over com-DBLP
(317,080 vertices, 1,049,866 edges) gave byte-identical community levels, and the whole pass over a
200,000-vertex planted partition gave a byte-identical written tree.

**What would reverse it.** The joint layout keeping fewer of the drawable edges short than the
per-type planes did, measured on the same multi-type corpus. That reopens how much a cross-type edge
weighs in the community pass; it does not bring back a plane per type.

## `program` is a design question

The manifest has the shape — `{"by": "program", "x": …, "y": …}` — and the writer emits only
`layout`. What is undecided is how a program says it: a clause in the language, checked like any other
(the columns exist, and are numeric, on at least one type), or a parameter the host passes to the
write. With a program position there is no community pass and no `cluster_id`; the ids are the
`ST_Hilbert` rank of the declared columns over the types that have them, and every other type is
absent.

**What settles it.** The first program that has coordinates of its own and needs them drawn — a
graph of places with `lon` and `lat`. Its author's program decides which of the two spellings reads
as fossil, and the writer grows exactly that one.

## What this page does not carry

No taxonomy of where coordinates came from — no CRS, no *derived* against *measured*, no
`coordinates:` list with a system per entry. `by` answers the one question a reader acts on — is this
the writer's picture or the data's — and a CRS is a property of the program's columns that the
program can name in a column of its own. The measured taxonomy, and the `channels:` block that
declared a colour column and its domain, are on [the derived plane](/docs/design/later/position-plane).

**What would bring a taxonomy back.** A reader that does something different for two programs whose
positions are both `program` — reprojects one, say. Then the difference is a field, and it is named
for what that reader does.
