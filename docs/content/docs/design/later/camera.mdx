A corpus is [addressed, not queried](/docs/design/corpus): a reader computes the address of every tile
it wants before it issues a request. [One door](/docs/design/one-door) is the shape that arithmetic is
published in. This page is the other side of it — what a *view* has to be, and what it owes whoever
is looking at the picture. The view is `@kanzo-tech/graph`, in kanzo-ui; what is argued here is what
the contract on this side has to make possible for it.

## Three things, kept apart

A renderer that pans across more data than it can hold keeps three states apart. The naming is a
game engine's, and the separation is the reason each part can be tested without the other two.

<div className="fd-steps">

**Residency — what is loaded.** A cache over tiles, keyed by `(type, z, tile)`, with an eviction
policy. It needs no camera: a set of addresses, the batches behind them, and a rule for what to
drop. It is the only part that asks the reader for anything.

**Visibility — what is drawn, out of what is loaded.** A zoom, a cull against the tile boxes
`tileMatrix` publishes, and a predicate. It needs no request at all: everything it decides, it
decides over numbers and batches already in hand.

**The camera — centre and zoom, canonical, with the rectangle derived.** Not a rectangle that
accumulates pan and zoom deltas. Two numbers and a derivation, so that "the same camera" is a
comparison of two numbers rather than of an accumulated transform.

</div>

**Under `frame` residency and visibility were one set, and that was measured.** Over the
million-vertex bench corpus the whole-extent view opened **245 tiles of 245** — 20.4 MB — to draw one
vertex in sixty-four, and every one of the 145 tiles the 50% view opened was already among them. The
reader answered a rectangle, so an answer could not be reused for the next one.

**A cache over bytes is not buildable at the reader's seam, and building one proved it.** DuckDB
issues `read_parquet` from inside its Worker; the thread that holds the reader never sees a tile's
bytes and has nothing to cache. What that thread can hold is **answers** — and
[the backend](/docs/design/backend#three-layers-and-what-each-owns) moved the seam so that an answer
is one tile, which makes the answer cache a tile cache after all, held by the view. The measured win
was the one latency predicted rather than the one bytes did: `@fossil-lang/draw`'s residency (deleted
with the playground on 2026-09-29) settled a rectangle already held in 3 ms against 175. The byte
half stays the engine's — `SET parquet_metadata_cache = true`, which `Engine` asks a host for.

**What would reverse it.** A reader that sees the bytes — a host that hands this package byte ranges
rather than an engine. Then a byte cache is buildable where the bytes are, and residency could move
under the reader; it would still be one cache, not two.

### The coarse zoom stays on screen while the fine one arrives

The interval between the camera moving and an answer arriving is the one residency buys back, with
the trick of not clearing the previous frame until the next one is ready: **a cell tile is drawn until
the row tiles under it arrive, and then it goes.** That is the one crossing
[cells](/docs/design/cells) admits, and the backend states it as a ruling — rows and cells cross only
while loading. What makes the parent *correct* to leave up rather than merely better than blank is
[monotone refinement](#2-monotone-refinement): the rows that replace a cell are exactly its members.

What was blank under `frame` was the region newly in frame, not the frame: a view that uploads only
when its state changes keeps the previous picture up, correctly placed, because positions are corpus
coordinates and the renderer is pinned — see [path independence](#3-path-independence).

## The three properties

The measurements below were taken against a real million-vertex corpus through a real engine, with
no browser and no GPU, by scripts in the playground that are deleted with it (after `2fa5f22`). What
they could not check was the picture. What they checked was every claim the picture rests on.

### 1. Fidelity — a coarse view is the same image with fewer points

For any region *R*, the share of the coarse view inside *R* is the share of the graph inside *R*. A
view that fails this is not slower or emptier: it is **wrong about the shape of the graph**. It shows
a cloud the corpus does not have, and nothing on screen says so.

The statistic is **total variation distance** between two normalised density grids. Total variation
and not a χ², because the question is about a picture: TV is exactly the largest fraction of the ink
that can land in the wrong place, it is bounded in [0, 1], and it does not grow with the grid the way
a χ² statistic does. **The tolerance is measured, not chosen**: the question is not "is the distance
small" but "is it larger than a subsample of the same size with no spatial structure at all".

Under `frame` the coarse view was a decimation along `dense_id`, and it beat an unstructured
subsample of its own size at every level — 3.8× closer at 250,000 marks, 1.4× at 3,907. The coarse
view is a rung of [the pyramid](/docs/design/cells) now, where a mark is a cell drawn at its members'
centroid, and the same statistic is taken there: rung by rung the margin over the null grows rather
than decays down the pyramid, **+0.1470** at rung 4's 310 marks on com-DBLP. Cells carries the table.

#### A fidelity measure has a resolution, and below one bin per mark it is measuring itself

**The grid is not part of the measure. It is a resolution chosen for the arms it was measured on, and
it does not travel.** The statistic is only reading the picture while the drawn set has at least as
many points as the grid has bins. Below that the drawn set cannot reach most cells, its distance from
the whole graph saturates towards 1 — and so does its null's, because the null is the same size. Two
numbers pinned against the same ceiling differ by their binning noise, and the sign of that
difference is not evidence of anything. **The quantity that says which regime you are in is
`bins/mark`**, and it belongs beside the verdict rather than left to be recomputed.

A coarse *summary* is exactly the artefact that breaks a fixed grid. Rung 4 of the pyramid on
com-DBLP delivers 310 marks, and read on a 48×48 grid — 7.43 bins per mark — it scores 0.8431 against a
null of 0.8415 and appears to fail. It does not: re-measured at about `sqrt(marks)` bins per axis,
where nothing is asked to fill more bins than it has points, the same rung beats the same null by
**+0.1470**.

**This was published as a fact about the pyramid before it was recognised as a fact about the grid**,
and the retraction is on [cells](/docs/design/cells#the-quotient-saturates-down-the-pyramid-and-the-marks-do-not).
Measured across two corpora at five resolutions, **no arm at one bin per mark or finer fails, and
every failure is at two bins per mark or coarser** — 130 rows, zero counterexamples in either
direction. So a fidelity figure quoted without the mark count it was taken at, and the grid it was
taken on, is not a figure.

**What would reverse it.** A statistic whose value does not depend on the grid — a distance between
point sets rather than between binned densities, measured to separate a structured sample from its
null as sharply as TV does at one bin per mark.

### 2. Monotone refinement — zooming in only adds

`{drawn at the closer zoom} ⊇ {drawn at the farther zoom} ∩ {inside the closer window}`, where a
coarse mark counts as drawn at the closer zoom when its members are.

Under `frame` it was bought by the decimation nesting — `dense_id % 4^(k+1) = 0` is a strict subset
of `dense_id % 4^k = 0` — and measured: one fixed rectangle at six consecutive levels lost and moved
nothing, and a real zoom path carried 100.0% of the previous picture across five steps.

Under the tile contract it is bought by the partition instead, and it is arithmetic rather than a
measurement: **a cell is `dense_id >> shift` and the partition is quaternary**, so the parent of
`(z, t)` is `(z − 1, t >> (shift(z−1) − shift(z)))`, a cell's rows at the zoom below are exactly its
members, and every member is placed where the payload places it. Zooming in replaces a cell with the
rows it summarised and never with something else. `crates/fossil-layout/tests/cells.rs`
holds the membership against the payload.

**What would reverse it.** A partition that is not a shift of `dense_id` — a base that is not a power
of four, or cells cut on something other than the order. Then a cell's members are a lookup and not a
range, and refinement is a claim to test per corpus rather than a property of the addressing.

### 3. Path independence — the same rectangle by two routes is the same picture

The one that was broken, and **the cause was not where it was looked for.**

`frame` was a pure function of `(rect, level)`, and `scan.read` is a pure function of an address, so a
route can only change the answer by changing **which addresses are asked for**. Under `frame` it
did, through the rectangle.

The renderer holds the positions it was handed and derives its space→screen map from *their extent*.
A camera reads back through `screenToSpacePosition`, which answers in that map's coordinates; the
corpus answers in the coordinates it was written in. cosmos.gl's `setPointPositions(positions)`
called with no second argument means, in its own documentation, *use `config.rescalePositions`* —
which, with the simulation off, is rescaling. So the map is rebuilt from whichever answer just
arrived, and the loop closes:

```
answer → new map → a different rectangle for the same camera → different tiles → …
```

Measured through the library's own scale accessor, with the camera set once and never touched:

| points uploaded | derived scale | rectangle the source is asked about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 15,625 over the extent | 1.2003 | x[175.7, 925.5] |
| 8,000 over a 30% box | 0.3334 | x[−798.8, 1900.5] |
| 2,500 over a 10% box | 1.0004 | x[101.1, 1000.7] |

One camera, three questions, **12.9× the area** between the first two. Driven over the
million-vertex corpus, two routes ending on the identical camera rectangle drew 8,395 marks one way
and **0** the other — the blank canvas that was reported — and pinned, both routes agreed exactly:
18,308 against 18,308, sharing 100% of the union.

The fix is one line of renderer configuration, and it is the same sentence as the one that turns the
force simulation off: **a renderer whose source hands back the coordinates its next query is expressed
in must not move them.** It belongs in the renderer, which is the view's. A canonical camera — centre
and zoom as the state, the rectangle derived on demand — is the more robust form of the same fix and
is worth having anyway: a route cannot accumulate error into the camera even where a renderer does
move the ground under it.

**What would reverse it.** A renderer that must rescale — one whose precision depends on positions
near its origin. Then the reader answers in a space the view declares, and the mapping is a declared
transform on both sides rather than one rebuilt from each answer.
