Every edge type is stored **twice, in one relation**:

- `by_source.parquet` — ordered by `src_dense`. This is CSR on disk.
- `by_target.parquet` — ordered by `dst_dense`. This is CSC on disk.

Verified on a ten-million-vertex corpus: `by_source.parquet` has **zero out-of-order rows** by
`src_dense` across 71,024,690 rows, and `by_target.parquet` likewise by `dst_dense`.

## Why the order is worth a convention

Because a reader that trusts it gets **wrong answers rather than slow ones** if it is not there.

A sorted adjacency file is not merely convenient to scan — it *is* the CSR structure a graph
algorithm wants, offsets and values, already laid out. Reading it as the CSR it claims to be needs
no degree-counting pre-pass, no scatter, and no intermediate bag of pairs. A file that claims the
order and does not have it does not fail: it builds a different graph, silently, and lays the corpus
out by it.

That is not hypothetical arithmetic. In the write path, taking the file as the CSR it already is
removed a `Vec<(u32, u32)>` of every edge (568 MB at ten million), a degree-counting pre-pass and an
80 MB cloned cursor scattering random writes over the 568 — **−1.71 GiB of process peak**, with the
corpus byte-identical. None of the three was ever asked for by the algorithm.

They existed because of a type. A constructor taking **offsets and targets cannot need a counting
pass**, because its signature already asserts the order; one taking a **bag of pairs has to rebuild
it**, because the bag asserts nothing. The write path took the bag, so the ordering the file already
had could not be passed through it. **An invariant lives in a type or it lives in a comment**, and
here the file is the type.

And the order is the expensive half, which is why it is worth spending a convention on rather than
leaving to whoever reads the file. Edge-centric streaming systems are built on one observation:
walking edges in sequence while holding only per-vertex state beats random access over a resident
adjacency, because sequential bandwidth is orders of magnitude larger than random. **X-Stream** and
**GraphChi**'s parallel sliding windows spend their machinery on *producing* that sequence — it is
the difficulty their designs exist to answer. Here it is not machinery: it is the convention this
page opens with, and the count at the top of the page is a corpus being held to it. A consumer
starts where those systems finish.

Guard: `csr-and-csc`. What it does not fix is the secondary order — rows sharing a `src_dense` may
appear in any order, and no reader may depend on which.

## One relation, stored twice

The two orientations carry the same edge set. The manifest carries **one** `edge_count` for the
relation rather than one per orientation, and that is the shape of the claim: each orientation
separately has to add up to that number. What it does not say is that the two hold the same *rows* —
same size and different contents is a reader answering a neighbourhood query from one and a count
from the other, and silently disagreeing with itself.

Guard: `one-relation-twice`, checked as a symmetric difference in both directions, so neither a
missing edge nor an extra one survives. What it cannot prove is that either orientation is the
*whole* relation: both being wrong identically passes the difference, and both being truncated by
the same amount passes the count.

And every endpoint addresses a vertex that exists, in its own type's dense space. That is the
corruption a renumbering can introduce and that no row count would reveal — the edge counts still
match, both orientations still agree, and the graph is wrong. Guard: `no-dangling-endpoint`.

## An edge lives in the tile of the endpoint its file is ordered by

Both orientations are tiled, and each is keyed by **the same range as the vertices** of the endpoint
it is ordered by: tile `k` under `by_source/` holds every edge whose `src_dense` is in vertex tile
`k` of the source type, and tile `k` under `by_target/` every edge whose `dst_dense` is in vertex
tile `k` of the *destination* type. On a cross-type edge those are two different `dense_id` spaces,
which is why `src_chunk_size` and `dst_chunk_size` are two fields.

A tile's row count is the total degree of those 4,096 vertices, not 4,096 — **`chunk_size` on an
edge is an addressing unit and never a row count.** A tile with no rows is not written, so a 404 is
the answer "these 4,096 vertices have no edges in this direction", and it costs nothing to give.

**A 404 is that answer and also "this tile was never uploaded", and nothing in the address separates
them.** The manifest's `edge_count` is what does: a reader sums the tiles it found against the
declared number, and a short sum is a corpus that is missing rows rather than a graph that is
sparse. The same hole exists on the vertex side and is closed the same way, with `vertex_count`
([Identity](/docs/format/conventions/identity)). Guard: `declared-count`, which checks each orientation
against the declared number on its own, because `one-relation-twice` compares the two to each other
and passes on both being truncated identically.

### Given a `dense_id`, where its edges are

This is the whole of what a reader needs, and every part of it comes out of the edge type's
manifest — nothing is agreed between a writer and a reader out of band:

| you want | read | at | filter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| the out-edges of `v` | `<edge prefix>by_source/tile{k}.parquet` | `k = v >> shift(src_chunk_size)` | `src_dense = v` |
| the in-edges of `v` | `<edge prefix>by_target/tile{k}.parquet` | `k = v >> shift(dst_chunk_size)` | `dst_dense = v` |

The manifest carries it as two `adj_lists` entries, each saying which endpoint column addresses it
and where its tiles are:

```yaml
prefix: edge/Person_knows_Person/
src_chunk_size: 4096
dst_chunk_size: 4096
adj_lists:
- ordered: true
  aligned_by: src
  prefix: by_source/
  file_type: parquet
- ordered: true
  aligned_by: dst
  prefix: by_target/
  file_type: parquet
```

`aligned_by` is the arithmetic — `src` means the rows in tile `k` are those whose `src_dense >>
shift` is `k` — and `prefix` is the URL, which is the one part a reader cannot compute. The shift
comes from `src_chunk_size` for the source-aligned list and `dst_chunk_size` for the target-aligned
one. A **hop** is therefore two reads: the tile `v` addresses in each direction, filtered to `v`'s
rows, far ends collected. The work follows the frontier and never the corpus.

### Why the target half is tiled, when drawing does not need it

It was not, and the reason it was not was sound for the question being asked: an edge with one
endpoint off screen has a visible segment that CSR does not bring, and that is a different question
from *drawing a window*, which CSR answers exactly ([below](#csr-not-lca)).

What reopened it is a second question with a measurement behind it. A **neighbourhood** — one hop
from a seed — was implemented in a reader the obvious way, as an undirected adjacency
(`src → dst UNION ALL dst → src`) under a `WITH RECURSIVE`. On 6.9M edges it **did not return**: one
hop from a single seed was still running after 45 seconds, and because that reader funnels every
query through one connection in FIFO, it took the page with it. The adjacency term materialises
13.8M rows before the recursion starts, and no `limit` on the answer bounds the cost of *finding*
it.

The shape is wrong rather than the tuning, and the right shape is the one this page already
describes — except that it needs both directions. **Following only the out-edges is a wrong answer
rather than a partial one:** "the papers by this author" is an in-edge from the author, and a
traversal that skips it reports a smaller graph rather than a slower one.

### What a hop costs, measured

Written by `apps/corpus/guards/fixture.mjs` at three sizes (`--layout files`, one file per tile, a
ring plus one chord per vertex, so two edges per vertex), and read with the `duckdb` CLI. Twelve
seeds spread evenly across the id space; the addressed and scanned answers are compared row for row
at every seed, so the two columns are the same answer arrived at differently.

**Bytes a hop reads** — the number that transfers, because it is what a reader over HTTP pays:

| N | edges | the two tiles the seed's id names | both relation files |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 250,000 | 500k | **113.0 kB** | 6.9 MB |
| 1,000,000 | 2.0M | **113.0 kB** | 27.5 MB |
| 4,000,000 | 8.0M | **113.0 kB** | 110.2 MB |

**The addressed hop is flat in N and the relation is not**, which is the whole claim: 61× less at a
quarter million, 975× at four million, and the gap is the corpus growing.

**Wall clock**, medians over the twelve seeds, all queries issued inside **one** `duckdb` process
because a process start is 10–40 ms on this machine and would otherwise be most of what is timed:

| N | addressed | scan the relation | `WITH RECURSIVE`, one hop |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 250,000 | 8 ms | 5 ms | 4 ms |
| 1,000,000 | 2 ms | 5 ms | 7 ms |
| 4,000,000 | **1 ms** | 16 ms | 23 ms |

<Callout type="warn" title="What this measurement is not">
**At a quarter million the addressed hop is the slowest of the three.** There is nothing to win on a
corpus a native engine reads in five milliseconds, and reporting only the four-million row would
hide that.

**And native DuckDB is the easy case for the other two.** It has every core of the machine and a
local file; the reader this was tiled for is single-threaded WASM over HTTP, which is where the same
recursive shape did not return in 45 seconds on 6.9M edges. So the milliseconds here are the
*floor* of the difference, and the byte column is the part that carries over.

**The graph is a ring with chords.** Every vertex has degree two, so no seed is a hub, and a
neighbourhood that fans out to a million rows is not measured at all.
</Callout>

The second tiling is the edge relation on disk a fourth time. At four million vertices it is
57.2 MiB against a corpus of 247.1 MiB without it — **+23%** — and it costs a drawing reader
nothing: a window still asks only `by_source`, and the target tiles are fetched by the traversal
that wants them.

Guards: `tile-of`, which checks each orientation's tiles against its own column and its own declared
chunk size; `declared-tiling`, which checks that an edge type's `src_chunk_size` equals its source
type's `chunk_size` and its `dst_chunk_size` its destination type's — a different number there would
address nothing — and that every declared `adj_list` carries a prefix; and `not-empty`, which is the
one that notices an orientation with a relation and no tiles, since every other guard here skips
what is not on disk.

### CSR, not LCA

The alternative placement is to hoist an edge to the deepest tile containing both endpoints — the
lowest common ancestor of the two ids, which with a fixed tile size is their common prefix. It is
the natural thing to reach for, and it is **dominated on both curves at once**.

Where the edges fall, by level. Level 0 is "both endpoints in the same leaf"; the root is the tile
every id shares:

| N | leaves | depth | in level 0 | in the root |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 200,000 | 49 | 6 | 88.51% | 24,942 |
| 1,000,000 | 245 | 8 | 80.72% | 98,429 |
| 5,000,000 | 1,221 | 11 | 74.42% | 292,410 |
| 10,000,000 | 2,442 | 12 | 73.33% | 621,102 |

**The root grows with N and the fraction that fits in a leaf shrinks.** And the root is paid by
every window, whether or not anything inside it is drawn, because a reader cannot know what it holds
without reading it — which is the definition of addressing.

What a window costs, mean of six:

| N | drawable | CSR reads | LCA reads | tiles CSR | tiles LCA | over-read CSR | LCA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 200,000 | 184,282 | 347,975 | 403,848 | 11.8 | 29.7 | 1.95× | 2.29× |
| 1,000,000 | 133,301 | 373,789 | 556,267 | 14.0 | 39.2 | 2.87× | 4.26× |
| 5,000,000 | 122,556 | 345,988 | 1,659,809 | 13.0 | 44.3 | 2.89× | 13.84× |
| 10,000,000 | 174,583 | 452,979 | 2,699,960 | 17.2 | 60.7 | 2.59× | 15.86× |

**CSR is flat and LCA is not.** CSR reads 346k to 453k edges while the corpus multiplies by fifty;
LCA goes from 404k to 2.70M. LCA also loses on requests, at 2.5×–3.5× the tiles.

Why, in one table — the first window at five million, level by level:

| level | tiles fetched | edges inside | of them drawable |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 14 | 318,043 | 108,036 |
| 1–2 | 15 | 31,048 | 5,793 |
| 3–8 | 19 | 511,345 | 2,422 |
| 9–11 | 4 | 977,000 | 3,852 |

The four high tiles move **53% of everything an LCA reader downloads and draw 0.39% of what they
contain**. That is not bad luck in one corpus: **near the root, the tree has no branching left to
prune with.** At five million there exist only three tiles at level 9, two at level 10 and one at
level 11, so any window touches almost all of them. The root at ten million is 621,102 edges of
which a window draws **1,170**.

Grouping levels in pairs — the quadrant, which is the natural move on a Morton order — merges
adjacent rows of that table without moving a single edge downward. The ceiling does not drop.

**And CSR needs no second request.** Every drawable edge has its source on screen, therefore in a
leaf the window already fetched: reading the window's leaves is an **exact superset** of what can be
drawn, with 1.1×–1.3× over-read at run granularity and 1.9×–2.9× at tile granularity.

<Callout title="What that comparison does not cover">
**"Drawable" means both endpoints on screen.** An edge with one endpoint outside has a visible
segment and neither placement brings it. Measuring that needs the CSC half, which is tiled — but it
is tiled for the traversal and not for this, and no figure on this page is a window that fetches
both. The number that would settle it is the same table one column wider: over-read and tiles per
window with `by_target` added, against the same six windows at each size.

**Six windows per size, not a distribution**, all at one zoom, square and centred on the median
vertex. **And the comparison is in rows, not bytes on the wire** — a high tile compresses *worse*
than a leaf, since its ids span a wider range and delta and RLE yield less, so the LCA figure is
optimistic if anything.
</Callout>

## The tiles are the relation

Splitting a file is where rows are silently dropped or written twice, and both survive every count
that is not a symmetric difference. `exactly-once` asserts that the tiles hold exactly the relation
they cut — the same count, and nothing on either side of the difference.
