Zoom far enough out of a million-vertex graph and the individual vertices stop being visible. What
is left is a distribution — dense here, empty there — and the question this page exists to answer is
whether that distribution is **about the graph** or about the program that drew it.

It is the second one more often than anybody admits, and nothing on the screen says which.

## The question, and the one fact it turns on

**Does a far view of a graph mean anything?** It depends on one thing and only one: where `x` and
`y` came from.

<div className="fd-steps">

**If the position is data** — a latitude and a longitude, a coordinate a model learned, anything
measured or fitted outside the renderer — then the plane is the world. Density is information.
Two vertices near each other are near each other in whatever space the data is about, a heat map of
where the vertices are is a statement about the domain, and a reader who zooms out learns something
true.

**If the position was invented by the layout** — phyllotaxis inside a grid cell, a force
simulation, anything a drawing algorithm chose because a picture needed coordinates — then the
density is about the algorithm. The plane is not a space; it is an arrangement. Zooming out
produces a picture of the arrangement's own regularities, and reading structure into it is reading
the output of the thing you were trying to see through.

</div>

The distinction is not subtle in its consequences and it is invisible in the artefact. Both cases
are two `float32` columns called `x` and `y`.

## What it was worth measuring

The far view is not hypothetical, and the case where it works was built and measured rather than
argued. Over com-DBLP — 317,080 authors, 1,049,866 undirected co-authorship edges — a complete
pyramid of density rasters is **183 kB across eleven levels**, exact at every level, and opens in
napari without a line of napari being written.

That is a real answer to a real question, and it is a correct one **only under the first case
above**. The same 183 kB over derived positions is a beautifully compressed picture of
`cluster_layout`'s grid.

## The defect, stated plainly

`crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/pass.rs, enrich_layout` computes positions **unconditionally**. It runs
`crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/community.rs, community_hierarchy` over the adjacency, partitions by the
finest level, places each community with
`crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/place.rs, cluster_layout`, and writes `x` and `y`.

A corpus that arrived carrying coordinates gets them overwritten, and no reader is told. The
manifest had no field in which the difference could be recorded even if the pass were willing to
record it: `crates/fossil-sinks/src/manifest.rs, VertexInfo` declared a list of properties, and a
property is a name and a type — `x` and `y` are two `float32` whether they are a latitude or a
phyllotaxis angle.

So the corpus could not answer the only question that decides whether its own geometry is worth
drawing far away. That is the whole of the finding, and the half of it that has landed is below.

<Callout type="info">
**The declaration is in; the refusal is not.** `VertexInfo` carries
`crates/fossil-sinks/src/manifest.rs, CoordinateSystem` now, and every corpus fossil writes
declares one: `name: layout`, over `x`/`y`, `provenance: derived`, `derived_by:
louvain+phyllotaxis`. It is declared in `crates/fossil-df/src/lib.rs, vertex_info` beside the
properties that make it possible — the same place and the same rule the identity index and the
pyramid are declared under, *the manifest is the plan and the pass is what fills it*.

What has **not** landed is the pass refusing to overwrite, and the reason is deliberate. Telling
`crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/pass.rs, enrich_layout` to leave a measured system alone means a
parameter on `VertexLayoutTarget`, and **nothing can set it**: no fossil program can supply its own
`x`, so the only corpus with a measured system is one some other writer made. Building the
parameter now would be [the error this project has made at nine
scales](/docs/design/discarded) — an extension point with one implementation and no second caller.
The condition is a producer, and it is a language question before it is a pass question.
</Callout>

## This is a defect with a precedent, and the precedent is the design

The repair is not novel here, because the identical defect was found and fixed one field over.

`crates/fossil-sinks/src/manifest.rs, Property` used to carry `is_nullable` and nothing about how
*many* values a predicate admits. A reader needing that answer invented one — *single*, because
single is the common case — and a corpus written from a shape declaring `+` read back as a
corpus declaring nothing, which read as a corpus declaring `1..1`. The fix was a declarative
field whose absence is honest:

> **`None` is a statement about the writer, not about the data.** A corpus whose types were
> inferred rather than declared has no cardinality to record, and so does one written before this
> field existed; both read back as "not declared" rather than as "single", which is the answer a
> default would have invented.
>
> — `crates/fossil-sinks/src/manifest.rs, Property`

Position had the same shape of defect and takes the same shape of repair — the same `Option`, the
same `skip_serializing_if`, the same rule about what absence means. Before it, a reader had to
invent *derived*, because derived is the common case; a corpus that arrived with latitudes read
back identically to one the layout pass invented; and the honest third state — **not declared** —
had nowhere to live.

## `coordinates:` — a list of systems, each with a provenance

A vertex type declares the coordinate systems it carries. Each names its two columns, and each
names **where it came from**:

| provenance | what it means | is the density information? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `geographic` | A coordinate reference system outside the corpus — latitude/longitude, a projected CRS | yes |
| `embedded` | A learned or fitted space — an embedding, an MDS solution, a UMAP | yes, about the embedding |
| `derived` | A drawing algorithm chose it because a picture needed coordinates | **no** |

**A list rather than a field**, and the reason is not symmetry. A corpus can legitimately carry
both: authors have affiliations with real locations *and* a community layout, and which one a
reader draws is the reader's business rather than the writer's. OME-NGFF reached the same shape for
the same reason — its `multiscales` entry carries `coordinateTransformations` as a list because one
array can sit in more than one space — and [prior art](/docs/design/prior-art) names it.

The cost is a permutation of **4 bytes per row per extra system**, which is a column and not a
corpus. Against it: the alternative is a corpus that silently answers a question it does not know
the answer to.

## What `derived` obliges, and the measurement that discharges it

A provenance of `derived` names an algorithm, and naming an algorithm is only worth anything if the
algorithm can be re-run. A position labelled *derived by X* that X does not reproduce is a label
with nothing behind it — and downstream, a holon whose referent moves between writes is not
navigable, which is why [holons](/docs/design/holons) treats this as its first property rather than
its last.

The reasoning was already in the code and had never been run as an experiment. `local_moving`
walks `0..n` in order and breaks ties with a strict `>` so the lower community id survives;
`Weighted::contract` carries a section titled *Why the output is bit-identical* explaining that its
counting sort is stable by construction; `densify` numbers communities by order of first
appearance. Every one of those is a comment asserting reproducibility, and none of them is a test.

Measured, 2026-09-11, two processes each, same binary:

| what was run | over | result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `community_hierarchy`, via `crates/fossil-layout/examples/hierarchy_stats.rs, read_edges` | com-DBLP, 317,080 vertices / 1,049,866 edges, 5 levels | all five levels' membership **and** quotient outputs byte-identical |
| the whole pass — `enrich_layout` — via `crates/fossil-layout/examples/enrich_memory.rs, planted` | 200,000 vertices at mean degree 10, 999,079 edges | the entire written tree byte-identical: the Morton-permuted payload, both adjacency orientations, the identity index, all five tiles and all three level directories |

Two graph shapes — a real co-authorship network and a planted partition — and both halves of the
pass. **The property holds.** What it does not yet have is a test that keeps it holding, and that is
the first thing this direction owes.

## What a derived plane turns out to look like

Everything above is about *whether* a coordinate system was derived. These two findings are about
what a derived one looks like once it is, and both were measured over the same com-DBLP — 317,080
authors, 1,049,866 undirected co-authorship edges. Neither is an argument against deriving
positions. Both are the same observation from the other side: a picture of the algorithm is exactly
what the far view shows, so it is worth knowing which algorithm, and at what resolution.

### 1 · The grid pitch is fixed by the largest group, and 82× of it is spare

`crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/place.rs, cluster_layout` places the clusters on the cells of a
grid in Z order and packs each cluster's members in phyllotaxis inside its own cell. The pitch is
`2 * INTRA_CLUSTER_RADIUS * sqrt(largest) + CLUSTER_SPACING`, with `INTRA_CLUSTER_RADIUS` at 12 and
`CLUSTER_SPACING` at 100 — and it is **one number for every cell**.

The partition that places is the finest level of Louvain, whose median group holds 3 members and
whose largest holds 20,459. So the disc term is sized for 20,459 in a grid whose median cell holds
three: √(20,459/3) ≈ **82× more radius than its occupant uses**, and every cell pays it.

| the placement grid | measured over com-DBLP |
| --- | --- |
| the pitch | 2·12·√20,459 + 100 ≈ **3,534**, uniform across every cell |
| the median group's disc | 2·12·√3 ≈ **41.6** |
| that disc against its own cell | **1.18%** of the width, **0.014%** of the area |
| every disc summed against the written extent | the plane is **0.046% occupied** — 99.95% empty |

The code says why the pitch is uniform, and the reason is a good one:

> The pitch is uniform rather than per-cluster because a cluster's *cell* must be findable from its
> id alone — that is what makes the placement a pure function of `cluster_ids` and reproducible
> without carrying a table.
>
> — `crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/place.rs, cluster_layout`

**That constraint stops holding the moment the cell pyramid is written** — the tree of summary rows
[holons](/docs/design/holons) is about. It is true of a corpus
whose only record of the partition is the two coordinate columns: with nothing else published, the
id is all a reader has, and a per-cluster size would need a table beside it. A pyramid that declares
a count per rung — `crates/fossil-sinks/src/manifest.rs, HolonRung` is the row that would carry one
— is that table, already published for another reason. A reader with the counts recomputes the
geometry rather than looking it up, and the placement stays a pure function of what the artefact
says.

Priced with a simulated buddy assignment giving every group a cell of its own size:

| | as placed | with a cell per group |
| --- | --- | --- |
| written extent | 560,176 | **63,640** — 8.8× more compact |
| occupancy | 0.046% | **3.5%** — 76× of the plane recovered |

**It does not make the pyramid cheaper, and saying so is half the finding.** The base rung stays
where it was — 25.7% of V before, 23.1% after — because the base is the placement partition either
way and compacting the plane does not merge a group with its neighbour. What improves is the
picture: 76× of the plane recovered is 76× of a far view that is looking at the graph rather than at
the grid's empty margins.

### 2 · The artefact publishes one partition and hides the one that drew it

`crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/pass.rs, enrich_layout_with` computes **two** partitions and
publishes one. `cluster_id` comes from
`crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/community.rs, flatten_to_budget` under `CLUSTER_BUDGET`, sized so a
drawing reader's aggregate stays under its row cap. The placement uses `levels.first()` — the finest
level — because the budget partition makes every community a root and leaves the ordering nothing to
put side by side. The pass argues both:

> `cluster_id` is what a drawing reader colours and aggregates by, one super-node per cluster under
> a row cap, so it must fit `CLUSTER_BUDGET` — which on this corpus means the top of the hierarchy.
> Placement wants the opposite: the finest level, whose communities are small enough that several
> fit in one window.
>
> — `crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/pass.rs, enrich_layout_with`

Each argument is correct on its own. What fails is that **only the first one reaches the artefact**.

| what a reader has against what drew the picture | measured over the written corpus |
| --- | --- |
| distinct `cluster_id` values written | **1,229** |
| groups the placement actually used | **~55,712**, filling 61,210 cells at cluster-cell scale |
| cells one written `cluster_id` spans | **14** at p50, **77** at p90, **3,445** at most |
| cells holding two different `cluster_id` values | **none, at any point in the corpus** |
| each `cluster_id` split per cell, width / (2·12·√m) | **0.83** at p50, **0.91** at p90 |

That last row is the confirmation rather than a curiosity: 0.83 and 0.91 are what `cluster_layout`
produces, so every per-cell subgroup is a phyllotaxis disc sized for its own membership. The
placement partition is not hiding somewhere abstract — it is legible in the coordinates, one cell at
a time, and the coordinates are the only place it exists at all.

The consequence for a reader is the whole of the finding. Colouring by `cluster_id` colours packets
of roughly 45 placement groups scattered across the plane, and nothing in the artefact says so.
**It is not the palette: the colour is not what is on the screen.** The palette row on [what was
discarded](/docs/design/discarded) asks for a slot per community, and a slot per community solves
the other half — running out of colours — while leaving this one exactly where it was.

And the repair is one rule, not two — but it is not the rule this page used to name. *The placement
partition **is** the base rung of the cell pyramid* was written before the pyramid was, and the
pyramid that got written partitions the `dense_id` axis at a declared base of sixteen vertices per
cell — `crates/fossil-sinks/src/manifest.rs, HolonTree` — which over com-DBLP is 19,818 cells
against the placement's 55,712 groups. They are not the same partition, and neither bends into the
other without giving up the gapless numbering or the arithmetic addressing. So the repair is not a
second artefact at all: it is a change to where the groups go, and it is the section below.

## The repair both findings have, and it is one rule

**Every group gets a cell of its own size.** A group of `m` members is given an aligned square of
the quaternary whose side is the next power of two above its own packing disc, and the squares are
handed out by bumping a Z-order frontier — a buddy allocator's addressing, over the allocation order
the hierarchy has already fixed.

`crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/place.rs, cluster_layout` keeps its signature and its purity. The
sizes a per-group cell needs are the ones it already counts in order to find `largest`, so the
placement stays a function of `cluster_ids` and of nothing else — which is what the objection in the
code was protecting, and it survives intact:

| | as placed | with a cell per group |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a group's cell | one of a uniform grid, pitch `2·12·√20,459 + 100` | an aligned square, side `2·12·√m` plus a proportional margin, rounded up to a power of two |
| where that cell is | `morton_decode(id)` — the id **is** the address | the Z-order frontier, bumped once per group in id order |
| the gap between two groups | 100 units, whatever the two groups are | a fraction of the disc it surrounds |
| what finds a group's cell | its id alone | its size, which the placement already counts |

Three decisions sit inside that, and each one is load-bearing.

### The area is the membership, because that is what makes the id axis mean something

A phyllotaxis disc of `m` members has an area proportional to `m`, so a cell sized to its own disc
puts the same number of vertices under every unit of plane. And `dense_id` is the **rank** of a
vertex in the Morton order of its position — `crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/morton.rs,
morton_ranks` — so a rank axis over a plane of uniform density *is* an area axis: an interval of `n`
ids covers `n/δ` of plane wherever on the plane it is taken.

Under the uniform pitch it covers whatever the emptiness there happens to be. Sixteen consecutive
ids inside the 20,459-member group are sixteen members of one phyllotaxis disc, a patch about 85
units across; sixteen consecutive ids out at the median are five whole groups in five whole grid
cells, better than 7,000 units across. **Same rung, same count, four orders of magnitude of area** —
which is the whole of why [a cell is claimed to be a region of the plane](/docs/design/holons) and
is not one yet.

<Callout type="warn" title="This is the idea that was discarded, and the difference is which layer it is applied at">
*Area proportional to population* is [a rejected row](/docs/design/discarded), and the evidence
against it is not retracted: allocating area by count produces a uniform field, which deletes
exactly the signal a far view is looked at for — where the graph is dense.

It does not bite here, and the reason is the first paragraph of this page. **There is no density
signal in a derived plane to delete.** Inside a group the members are already packed at one fixed
density — `INTRA_CLUSTER_RADIUS` is a constant, so a disc's area is already proportional to its
membership — and outside them the plane is empty margin sized by the largest group. Today's far view
is a regular lattice of dots on a field that is 99.95% nothing; it encodes population as dot size
and encodes the graph nowhere. What the packing trades is not signal for uniformity. It is
**emptiness** for uniformity.

The rejected row is about *encoding*: choosing area to mean population in a picture where area could
have meant something a layout found. This is about *packing*, and the signal the coarse view carries
is unchanged either way — a cell's `count` is `4^k` by construction at every rung and always was, so
what a mark says is its mode and its purity, which is what [holons](/docs/design/holons) already
argues it says.

The row comes back the day a placement exists whose extent means something — a force layout over a
level's quotient, say, where a community's spread is a fact about the graph. Packing that into
buddy blocks would be the discarded idea, and this page would be the one that was wrong.
</Callout>

### The margin is a ratio, not a constant

`crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/place.rs, CLUSTER_SPACING` is 100 units of a plane whose unit means
nothing — every coordinate here is `derived`, which is the whole of this page — and a constant added
to a side that scales as `√m` is a margin that eats the small end. A per-group cell with the
constant kept spends **93% of its area on margin at the median group and 26% at the largest**: the
same defect as the uniform pitch in a smaller denomination, an 11× density difference between the
two ends of the partition. A ratio spends the same fraction at both ends, and that is what makes the
rank axis flat rather than nearly flat.

### The frontier never goes back

A buddy allocator normally reuses the holes its alignment leaves, and that is refused here.
`crates/fossil-layout/src/layout/community.rs, order_by_hierarchy` numbers the placement groups by a
depth-first walk of the dendrogram, so **a run of consecutive group ids is a subtree**; a monotone
frontier turns that into a run of consecutive blocks, hence a run of consecutive Morton codes, hence
a contiguous interval of `dense_id`. Reusing a hole behind the frontier puts a later group in front
of an earlier one and breaks exactly that. The price is alignment fragmentation, and it is paid out
of a plane that is 99.95% empty.

### What it makes true

**Every partition in the artefact becomes an interval of `dense_id`.** A group is a contiguous
interval because its members share a block; a `cluster_id` is a contiguous interval because it is a
union of consecutive groups; every level of the dendrogram between them is one for the same reason.
That is the corpus's own addressing rule — `/docs/format` has no listings and no discovery because
an address is arithmetic — reaching the one partition that was outside it.

It is also the second finding's repair, **and it publishes nothing new to get there**. A reader
colouring by `cluster_id` was colouring 45 placement groups scattered over a 158×158 grid of nearly
empty cells. It will be colouring one region. The colour becomes what is on the screen, which is
what that finding said it was not, and the partition doing it is the one that was in the manifest
all along.

### What the implementation owes

Four things, and none of them optional:

- **The plane, re-measured.** The simulation above priced a cell per group at 8.8× the extent and
  76× the occupancy, and it did not pay alignment fragmentation. The implementation's own figures
  replace those rather than confirming them.
- **The spread of a cell's area, per rung.** This is the claim, and it is the number that can
  falsify it: a texel is an area, so a rung whose cells still differ in area by orders of magnitude
  is a rung of something other than texels.
- **The base rung's cost, which must not move.** The pyramid is `ceil(V / 16)` cells whatever the
  placement does. A repair that made it cheaper would have repaired something else.
- **A guard that a stranger can evaluate**: every `cluster_id` is a contiguous interval of
  `dense_id` — `max − min + 1 = count`, per group, over the written payload. It goes red for a
  hole-reusing allocator and for a placement that ignores the hierarchy's order, which are the two
  ways this gets got wrong.

## What this does not settle

**Which system a reader draws when a type declares two.** The list makes the choice expressible and
does not make it. A default ("the first") is a writer deciding a reader's question, which is the
mistake this page is about; naming one in the manifest is a third thing again.

**What a reader colours by, sizes by, or labels with.** The same observation this page opens with
— *a property is a name and a type* — is true of every channel and not only of the plane.
`crates/fossil-sinks/src/manifest.rs, Property` carries a name, a `data_type` and `is_primary`, so
there is no field in which a writer could say *this column is the community*, and the argument above
says it should not grow one: a default is a writer deciding a reader's question, and which column
means something to look at is the reader's question twice over.

So a reader decides, and the one reader in this repository decides in
`apps/playground/src/encoding.ts, encodingFor` — out loud, in one module, rather than as string
literals at three call sites, which is what it was. The rule it uses splits along the same seam
`openCorpus` already draws between the manifest's vocabulary and the payload's. **The bytes say what
is drawable:** the layout pass's `cluster_id` is declared by no `properties:` entry, so a derivation
that believed the manifest would find no column to colour a corpus that has one. **The manifest says
which is interesting:** of the columns a binned histogram is the right form for, the one
`quasi_identifiers` names is the one whose distribution the corpus itself says is worth a reader's
attention. Neither half is asked the other's question, and
`apps/playground/scripts/verify-encoding.mjs` holds both against the bench corpus — including that
the derived colour and `FrameParams.fill`'s own default answer one rectangle with identical
categories.

What is *not* decided by a reader is size, and that is a limit of the door rather than of the
artefact: `Corpus.frame` carries one categorical per row and no measure, so the streaming path has
nothing to spend a radius ramp on and the baseline beside it would be the only half of a comparison
that had one. Every level file already carries every column, so what would change it is a second
column on `FrameParams` and a second array on `Frame` — a projection, not an artefact — and nothing
has asked for one.

**Whether `derived` should name a version.** *Derived by Louvain and phyllotaxis* is not
reproducible across a change to either. A version string is the obvious answer and it is not
obviously the right one: it goes stale in the opposite direction, marking a corpus as
irreproducible when nothing that mattered changed. What would settle it is one instance of a
layout change that moved positions, with somebody needing to know.

**Whether the far view gets a third artefact.** A density raster is not a subset of rows, so it is
not a level — [the reference viewer](/docs/design/reference-viewer) sets out why a level is a
decimation and what a summary would have to be instead. The 183 kB above was measured outside the
corpus, and bringing it inside is a separate decision from this one.
