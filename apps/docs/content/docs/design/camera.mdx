A corpus is [addressed, not queried](/docs/design/corpus): a reader computes the URL of every tile
it wants before it issues a request. [One door](/docs/design/one-door) is the shape that arithmetic
is published in. This page is the other side of it — what a *reader* has to be, and what it owes
whoever is looking at the picture.

## Three things, and today two of them are one

A renderer that pans across more data than it can hold keeps three states apart. The naming is a
game engine's, and the separation is the reason each part can be tested without the other two.

<div className="fd-steps">

**Residency — what is loaded.** A cache over tiles, with an eviction policy. It needs no engine and
no camera: it is a set of tile numbers, the bytes behind them, and a rule for what to drop. It is
the only part that touches the network.

**Visibility — what is drawn, out of what is loaded.** A level, a rectangle, and a predicate. It
needs no network at all: everything it decides, it decides over bytes already in hand.

**The camera — centre and zoom, canonical, with the rectangle derived.** Not a rectangle that
accumulates pan and zoom deltas. Two numbers and a derivation, so that "the same camera" is a
comparison of two numbers rather than of an accumulated transform.

</div>

**Today residency and visibility are the same set, and the cost of that is measurable.** Over the
million-vertex bench corpus, four camera rectangles, a canvas of twenty thousand pixels:

| window | level | tiles opened | runs | bytes | drawn | matched |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| whole extent | 6 | **245 / 245** | 1 | 21,402,452 | 15,625 | 1,000,000 |
| 50% | 5 | 145 / 245 | 4 | 12,805,975 | 18,308 | 585,828 |
| 25% | 4 | 47 / 245 | 4 | 4,159,450 | 11,008 | 176,145 |
| 10% | 2 | 15 / 245 | 4 | 1,325,310 | 7,105 | 28,372 |

The first row is the whole corpus opened — 20.4 MB — to draw one vertex in sixty-four. It is not a
bug in the addressing: at the whole extent every tile *does* intersect the rectangle, and the
predicate `dense_id % 64 = 0` is spread across all of them. It is the absence of a level artefact
crossed with the absence of a residency notion, and the two fixes are independent.

The second row looks like the argument for the separation, in one number: **every one of the 145
tiles the 50% view opens was already among the 245 the whole-extent view opened.** A reader with
residency draws that frame from what it holds, and re-reads nothing.

<Callout type="warn">
**That is a claim about bytes and the bytes here are not weighed.** Every figure in the `bytes`
column is footer arithmetic — the size of the tiles the rectangle selected — and
`apps/playground/src/tiles.ts` says why it can be nothing else: DuckDB issues the real requests from
inside its Worker, where the calling thread cannot see them. So the column says what those tiles
*weigh*, never what crossed the wire.

And the engine already caches. `apps/playground/src/duckdb.ts, registerUrl` passes `directIO =
false` with the reason written beside it — *DuckDB caches what it has already read, which is what
makes the footer bought-once rather than bought-per-query*. If that cache also spans the payload
reads the canvas issues, the 12,805,975 of row two was **banked before residency existed**, and
building a cache in front of it buys nothing it does not already have.

Nothing in this tree settles that, because no instrument here weighs a request. What would settle
it is one measurement of the same rectangle asked twice with the network observed — which is the
only reading that can tell a cache hit from a re-fetch.

**The separation survives the doubt; its justification changes.** What is NOT in question is that a
read answered from cache still costs a round trip through the Worker before anything reaches the
screen. The case for residency is *latency*, and it stands on its own.

**Building it settled the rest, and against this page.** *A cache over tiles, the only part that
touches the network* — the way residency is described below — **is not buildable at this seam at
all.** DuckDB issues `read_parquet` from inside its Worker; the thread that holds a `BoundedSource`
never sees a tile's bytes and has nothing to cache. So the 3.3× is not this layer's to bank: it is
the engine's, which is precisely what `directIO = false` is there for.

What this thread can hold is **answers** — a rectangle already asked about, and the rows that came
back. That is what `packages/draw/src/residency.ts` holds, and the measured win is the one
latency predicted rather than the one bytes did: a rectangle already held settles in 3 ms against
175, and the frames in between get an interim in 8 to 18 ms.
</Callout>

A reader without the separation re-issues the read, and the interval between the camera moving and
the answer arriving is an interval with nothing on screen.

That interval is what the separation buys, and it is the same trick as not clearing the previous
frame until the next one is ready: **the coarse level stays on screen while the fine one arrives.**
Zarr's readers call it progressive refinement and it is only expressible if "what is drawn" and
"what is loaded" are two sets. [Monotone refinement](#2-monotone-refinement) is what makes the old
frame *correct* to leave up rather than merely better than blank: every vertex it drew is still
drawn, in the same place, at the finer level.

**And the seam already carries it, which was not obvious.** `BoundedSource` looks one-shot —
`slice(request)` returns one `Slice` and a caller awaits it — so a source has no way to say *here is
something now, and something better shortly*. It has one: `watch`, whose own contract is **a whole
slice rather than a nudge to ask again**. It was added so a source inside a crossfilter could be
told by the coordinator instead of re-asked, and the shape it needed for that is exactly the shape
progressive refinement needs — with no change to the renderer and no second method.

**Which way round, though, is decided by what reads the return value, and this page had it
backwards.** It said the coarse frame could be the return and the refined one arrive through
`watch`. It cannot: `slice` is a pure function of `(rect, level)`, [path
independence](#3-path-independence) is measured against its RETURN, and
`apps/playground/scripts/verify-properties.mjs` and `measure-frame.mjs` both read that return as
the answer. So **`slice` returns the settled answer and `watch` carries the interim** — which lands
on screen exactly like a returned one, because `useQueryLoop` registers its own setter as the
callback, and is superseded rather than merged.

Measured over the bench corpus with residency in front: an interim lands in **8 to 18 ms** where
the settled answer takes 56 to 244, and a rectangle already held — the same one the path opened on
— settles in **3 ms against 175**.

<Callout type="warn">
**And the blank interval this page describes is not what the app does.** It said the wait between
a camera moving and an answer arriving is *an interval with nothing on screen*. `useQueryLoop`
uploads only when the slice state changes, so the previous frame stays up, correctly placed —
positions are corpus coordinates and `PinFrame` stops the renderer rescaling them. What is actually
blank is the region newly in frame, which is the part the camera just uncovered.

The latency argument survives and the sentence overstated it. It also decides a design question:
an interim carrying points alone would *remove* the links already on screen, so an interim carries
its edges.
</Callout>

## The three properties

`apps/playground/scripts/verify-properties.mjs` is the measurement, and it runs against a real
million-vertex corpus on disk through a real engine — no browser, no GPU, no
`requestAnimationFrame`. What it cannot check is the picture. What it checks is every claim the
picture rests on.

### 1. Fidelity — a coarse view is the same image with fewer points

For any region *R*, `|level_k ∩ R| / |level_k|` ≈ `|level_0 ∩ R| / |level_0|`. A view that fails
this is not slower or emptier: it is **wrong about the shape of the graph**. It shows a cloud the
corpus does not have, and nothing on screen says so.

The statistic is **total variation distance** between two normalised density grids, 48×48 over the
extent. Total variation and not a χ², because the question is about a picture: TV is exactly the
largest fraction of the ink that can land in the wrong place, it is bounded in [0, 1], and it does
not grow with the grid the way a χ² statistic does.

**The tolerance is measured, not chosen.** Any subsample of size *n* carries sampling noise, so the
question is not "is the distance small" but "is it larger than a subsample of the same size with no
spatial structure at all". The null is `hash(dense_id) % 4^k = 0` — the same population, drawn
pseudo-randomly instead of by the Morton stride, over the same grid.

| level | drawn | TV from level 0 | random subsample of the same size | ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 250,000 | **0.0073** | 0.0277 | 3.8× closer |
| 4 | 62,500 | **0.0227** | 0.0634 | 2.8× |
| 6 | 15,625 | **0.0644** | 0.1326 | 2.1× |
| 8 | 3,907 | **0.1727** | 0.2489 | 1.4× |

The Morton decimation beats an unstructured subsample of its own size at every level, which is the
form the claim has to take: it is not "the error is small", it is "the error is smaller than the
error you cannot avoid".

Over regions rather than cells — eight window/level pairs, windows from 50% down to 5% of the extent
— the share of the drawn set landing inside a window is within **1.54%** of the share of the whole
graph. Worst case is the 5% window at level 4: **0.720% against 0.709%**. The band it is checked
against is measured too: at the smallest region and coarsest level tested the level-0 share is over
a thousand vertices, and a binomial of that size has a relative standard error of about 3%.

#### A fidelity measure has a resolution, and below one bin per mark it is measuring itself

**The 48×48 is not part of the measure. It is a resolution chosen for the arms in the table above,
and it does not travel.** The statistic is only reading the picture while the drawn set has at least
as many points as the grid has bins. Below that the drawn set cannot reach most cells, its distance
from the whole graph saturates towards 1 — and so does its null's, because the null is the same size.
Two numbers pinned against the same ceiling differ by their binning noise, and the sign of that
difference is not evidence of anything. **The quantity that says which regime you are in is
`bins/mark`**, and it belongs beside the verdict rather than left to be recomputed.

The table above is safe on that rule with room to spare: its coarsest row draws 3,907 marks into
2,304 bins, **0.59 bins per mark**. A coarse *summary* is exactly the artefact that breaks it. A cell
of [the pyramid](/docs/design/cells) at rung 4 on com-DBLP delivers 310 marks — 7.43 bins per mark —
and read on this grid it scores 0.8431 against a null of 0.8415 and appears to fail. It does not:
re-measured at about `sqrt(marks)` bins per axis, where nothing is asked to fill more bins than it
has points, the same rung on the same file beats the same null by **+0.1470** — a wider margin than
rung 1 gets at 48×48.

**This was published as a fact about the pyramid before it was recognised as a fact about the grid**,
and the retraction is on [cells](/docs/design/cells#the-quotient-saturates-down-the-pyramid-and-the-marks-do-not).
The tell was in the numbers the whole time: a gap that collapses from ~0.1 to ~0.002 and then holds at
~0.002 for six rungs running is a saturated statistic, not six artefacts losing information at six
different scales. `crates/fossil-layout/tests/level_vs_rung.rs, resolution_sweep` is the instrument
that separates the two, and it is kept in the tree rather than retired with its conclusion: measured
across two corpora at five resolutions, **no arm at one bin per mark or finer fails, and every failure
is at two bins per mark or coarser** — 130 rows, zero counterexamples in either direction.

So a fidelity figure quoted without the mark count it was taken at, and the grid it was taken on, is
not a figure. Every one on this page has both.

### 2. Monotone refinement — zooming in only adds

`{drawn at the closer zoom} ⊇ {drawn at the farther zoom} ∩ {inside the closer window}`.

This is the property that makes a zoom *refine* the picture instead of replacing it, and it is
bought by quantising the stride **up to a power of two**: `dense_id % 4^(k+1) = 0` is a strict subset
of `dense_id % 4^k = 0`, so a vertex drawn once stays drawn, in the same place, at every closer
zoom. An arbitrary `ceil(matched / limit)` does not nest — the multiples of 37 and of 21 meet only
at 777 — and the price of the quantisation is at most one extra halving of the sample.

In the door's own terms, one fixed rectangle at six consecutive levels:

| step | vertices carried | lost | moved |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 → 5 | 6,310 | 0 | 0 |
| 5 → 4 | 12,625 | 0 | 0 |
| 4 → 3 | 25,244 | 0 | 0 |
| 3 → 2 | 50,516 | 0 | 0 |
| 2 → 1 | 101,058 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 → 0 | 202,126 | 0 | 0 |

And in the camera's terms — a real zoom path, where the window shrinks as well as the level
changing, so a vertex may leave the frame honestly — `verify-nesting.mjs` carries **100.0%** of the
previous picture across every one of five steps, counting only the vertices still inside the tighter
window so that every loss would be resampling rather than leaving the frame.

The two forms are one property and it is asserted once: the camera-path script is *run* from the
property suite rather than restated in it.

### 3. Path independence — the same rectangle by two routes is the same picture

The one that was broken, and **the cause was not where it was looked for.**

`frame` is a pure function of `(rect, level)` — the same rectangle at level 3 asked twice with the
whole corpus queried in between returns the same 73,228 marks. So a route can only change the answer
by changing the **rectangle**. And it did.

The renderer holds the positions it was handed and derives its space→screen map from *their extent*.
A camera reads back through `screenToSpacePosition`, which answers in that map's coordinates; the
corpus answers in the coordinates it was written in. cosmos.gl's `setPointPositions(positions)`
called with no second argument means, in its own documentation, *use `config.rescalePositions`* —
which, with the simulation off, is rescaling. So the map is rebuilt from whichever answer just
arrived, and the loop closes:

```
frame → answer → new map → a different rectangle for the same camera → …
```

Measured through the library's own scale accessor, with the camera set once and never touched:

| points uploaded | derived scale | rectangle the source is asked about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 15,625 over the extent | 1.2003 | x[175.7, 925.5] |
| 8,000 over a 30% box | 0.3334 | x[−798.8, 1900.5] |
| 2,500 over a 10% box | 1.0004 | x[101.1, 1000.7] |

One camera, three questions, **12.9× the area** between the first two. The predicted factors from
the transcribed arithmetic are 1.2000 / 0.3333 / 1.0000, which is why the transcription is trusted.

Driven over the million-vertex corpus, two routes ending on the *identical* camera rectangle — the
same object, so nothing here is a floating-point argument:

| | rectangle asked about | level | drawn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| rescaled, straight in | x[−981, 347] | 4 | 8,395 |
| rescaled, wandering | x[−3719, −1022] | 0 | **0** |
| pinned, either route | x[−253, 1355] | 5 | 18,308 |

The second row is the blank canvas that was reported. Its derived scale walks 1.0000 → 1.2104 →
4.8441 → 1.6247 → 0.5963 across five camera moves, and the rectangle walks off the corpus with it.
Pinned, both routes agree exactly: 18,308 against 18,308, sharing 100% of the union.

The fix is one line of renderer configuration, and it is the same sentence as the one that turns the
force simulation off: **a renderer whose source hands back the coordinates its next query is
expressed in must not move them.** It belongs upstream, in the renderer, rather than in a consumer
pinning it from a child component.

**A canonical camera is the more robust form of the same fix and is worth having anyway.** Centre
and zoom as the state, with the rectangle derived on demand, means a route cannot accumulate error
into the camera even where a renderer does move the ground under it — and it puts the whole thing in
pure arithmetic testable with no browser. It was not, however, the cause here: the app accumulates
no deltas of its own. The rectangle was correct arithmetic over a moving definition of the space.

## What this reads is what the app is

The property suite does not take a flag to decide which state the app is in. It reads the app's own
source and derives the answer from the string that does the pinning, so the verdict is what the app
**is** rather than what a flag says it is. A flag that reports what the code does is a second copy of
the fact, and the two copies drift in the direction nobody tests.
