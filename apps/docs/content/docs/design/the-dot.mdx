fossil has one access operator, and it means one thing.

> **`.` means *member of*, and the type on the left decides what is on the right.**

| On the left | Its members are |
| --- | --- |
| a **namespace** — `io`, `str`, `parse`, `clean` | its functions |
| a **type** — `User`, `Person` | its fields |
| a **relation** — `Users`, `Adults` | its operations — `where`, `select`, `join` |

That is the entire access story. `User.email` is a field of a type; `str.lower` is a function of a
namespace; `Adults.where(…)` is an operation of a relation. Reading any fossil expression requires
knowing what is on the left, and nothing else.

## Why there is no second operator

The obvious alternative is Rust's, where `::` walks a path known at compile time and `.` accesses a
value at run time. It is a good distinction in Rust. It has **no referent here**.

A fossil program does not execute. It is elaborated into
[an operator tree](/docs/design/algebra) and lowered; `User.age` is not a field read at run time,
it is a column reference that becomes SQL. There is no moment at which one side of that distinction
is happening and the other is not, so importing the two operators would import a difference the
language does not have — and a reader would have to learn a rule whose justification they can never
observe.

One operator, and the type answers.

## Verbs are rows, not grammar

`where`, `select` and `join` are not keywords. They are catalogue entries whose receiver type is a
relation, in exactly the way `lower` is a catalogue entry whose receiver type is a string. Nothing
about them is special except what they operate on.

The consequence is the point: **a new capability is a row, never a production.** Adding an operation
over relations does not touch the grammar, the parser, the checker's dispatch or the editor's
completion, because none of those know the names of verbs — they know how to ask a type what it has.
That is the same property [the provider registry](/docs/design/type-providers) has, and the same one
that lets [the standard library be generic](/docs/design/generic-functions), and it is not three
properties. It is one, seen from three sides.

It also removes an ambiguity the language would otherwise have to carry. When a namespace is
something that is not a value, `clean.slug` without parentheses has to mean "a named function, not
applied", and that is a special case in the parser justified entirely by the namespace's oddity. With
typed members, there is no such case to write.

## `:=` binds, `=` assigns

Two glyphs, and the distinction is real rather than decorative.

`:=` introduces a name — a shape binding, a source binding, a derived relation. What kind of thing is
being bound is said by the left-hand side, not by the operator, so one binder suffices for all of
them.

`=` is what it looks like everywhere else: an assignment inside a mapping body, including the
identity slot. The line `name = User.name` puts a value into a slot the shape declared; it does not
introduce a name.

The rule to hold on to is that a binder appears where something enters scope and an assignment
appears where a slot gets filled. Those are different acts, and a language that spells them the same
way makes the reader work out which is happening from context.

## The cost, stated as a cost

This design has one price, and it is paid in the corpus rather than in the compiler.

**One catalogue entry is reachable by two paths.**

Those two lines call the same entries. `Contact.email.trim()` reaches `trim` through the value;
`str.trim(Contact.name)` reaches the same `trim` through the type. Both are correct, both will
appear in real programs, and anybody reading a corpus of fossil programs will see both spellings of
the same idea.

The house rule against two ways of saying one thing is a real rule, and this is a genuine exception
to the letter of it. What makes it acceptable is that it is a **resolution rule** rather than two
constructs: there is one entry, one signature, one implementation, and two ways to name the same
member — which is precisely `s.len()` ≡ `str::len(&s)` in Rust and `s.upper()` ≡ `str.upper(s)` in
Python, the two languages the largest number of programmers read. The cost is that it has to be
explained once, and it is explained once, in
[the book's page on expressions](/docs/book/expressions).

## What would reverse this

Three observations, in the order they would arrive.

**1. A type whose set of members cannot be decided statically.** The entire design rests on the
receiver being known at compile time. A receiver whose members depend on data — a source format
whose columns vary per row, a dynamically assembled record — would break not the syntax but the
premise underneath it, and the answer would have to be a different one.

**2. The two paths of the resolution rule being used for different things.** The moment an idiom
appears where `str.lower(x)` means something `x.lower()` does not, they stop being one entry reached
two ways and become two spellings — which is the thing the house rule actually prohibits. This is
observable in the corpus rather than arguable: it is a question about what people wrote, and the
corpus is where to look.

**3. A real vocabulary whose short names collide so often that renaming becomes the norm.** Property
keys are the last segment of a predicate IRI, and
[the rename](/docs/design/type-providers) exists for the occasional collision between vocabularies.
If a working program needs a rename on most of its properties, the short name has stopped being the
name, and the argument for bare keys goes with it.

## What is still open

Two questions this design raises and does not close, stated as questions rather than as gaps.

**Where `select` lives when the relation came from a join.** A join produces a relation whose row
type is the union of two schemas; projecting from it is well defined, and *how it is written* — which
side qualifies, and whether the qualification survives into the mapping body — is a surface question
with more than one defensible answer.

**Whether `io` should also name the destination.** Sources are named by a provider in the program;
the destination is an input of the command that runs it. There is a real argument on each side — a
destination is an operational decision belonging to whoever launches the job, and it is also the one
part of a program's meaning that lives outside the program. What would settle it is a program whose
output location is part of what makes it that program rather than another one.
