title: What was discarded
description: The alternatives that were considered and rejected, each with why it is attractive, the evidence against it, and the observation that would bring it back — grouped by the argument each one is the shadow of.
---

Every design decision has a shadow: the thing that was not done. A decision recorded without its
shadow is unfalsifiable, and a shadow recorded without **what would reverse it** is a graveyard.

So every entry here has four fields and no more — **the idea**, **why it is attractive**, **the
evidence against it**, and **what would bring it back**. An entry that cannot state its last field
does not go on the page; that is the admission rule, and it is what stops this from becoming a list
of things somebody once disliked.

This is the one page here you are expected to search rather than read.

## Shadows of the algebra and the checker

### Rewriting the operator tree before it is lowered

**The idea.** A set of algebraic rewrite rules in the middle of the compiler — pushing filters down,
eliminating redundant projections, folding constants — before anything is emitted.
**Why it is attractive.** It is what a compiler middle-end is *for*, and the rules are individually
correct.
**The evidence against it.** They competed with an optimiser that is already underneath, in the
engine that runs the query, and they lost by definition: the engine sees the whole plan plus
statistics the middle does not have. Removing them is not declining to optimise; it is declining to
optimise **twice**, in the layer with less information.
**What would bring it back.** A transformation that depends on something only the type layer knows
and the engine cannot recover from the SQL it is handed.

### Typing a join's `on` condition by running the body checker over it

**The idea.** The join condition is an expression and the algebra already demands `Bool` of a
`Filter` predicate, so type the `on` predicate the same way: synthesise it against the joined row and
require `Bool`. That closes the untyped-join-key gap as a special case of a general rule instead of
as a special case.
**Why it is attractive.** It is one rule rather than two, it is the rule the algebra page already
states, and it catches every malformed condition rather than only the equality.
**The evidence against it.** An ordering, and it is structural rather than a matter of taste. The row
algebra runs **before** the body checker and produces its input: the scope a join hands on is what
every subsequent expression is synthesised against, so the pipeline cannot call the synthesiser
without the synthesiser's own input being the thing it is trying to compute. Building it means a
second expression typer over a scope rather than a row, which is a duplicate of the one that exists —
and duplicating a type checker to close a gap that one comparison closes is the wrong size of change.
The narrow rule is also the one the backend already needs: the plan builder destructures the
condition into exactly `equality of two column references` and refuses everything else structurally,
so the typed check is the earlier, better-worded twin of a refusal that already happens.
**What would bring it back.** A join condition that is legitimately **not** a single equality — a
range join, a conjunction of two keys, a condition with a function call on one side. The narrow rule
has nothing to say about any of them, and the first program that wants one is the program that makes
the general rule cheaper than the exceptions.

### Width subtyping on records

**The idea.** Let a record with more fields be accepted where one with fewer is expected — the
ordinary structural-subtyping rule.
**Why it is attractive.** It is what most structural type systems do, and it makes projections
compose without ceremony.
**The evidence against it.** None was needed: no program required it, and the invariant rule is
strictly simpler to explain and to check. It is a decision to stay narrow, not a rejected argument.
**What would bring it back.** A demand signal, named in advance: partial-row projections flowing
through the standard library, where a caller legitimately holds a wider row than the signature asks
for.

## Shadows of type providers

### Declaring the source's schema outside the program

**The idea.** Attach the schema — or the shape document — to the *invocation* rather than to the
program: a command-line flag, an attached file, a setting in the host.
**Why it is attractive.** A schema feels operational, and keeping it out of the program lets one
program be pointed at several environments.
**The evidence against it.** An adversarial reading went looking for the retrospective that regrets
putting providers in the program and could not find one. What it found is that the documented damage
in that family is about **liveness, not location** — invisible drift, an invalidation signal the
batch compiler ignores — and that the systems which moved the declaration *out* did not come out
better: one requires a live database at build time and fixes it by committing an anchor rather than
by relocating the declaration; another keeps a generated schema file in the repository and pays for
two sources of truth. And the decisive criterion is independent of all of that: **a program whose
meaning changes with an external flag is not self-contained.**
**What would bring it back.** A host that must compile one unmodified program against two schema
revisions and legitimately cannot express that as two programs.

### Disambiguating a derived name automatically

**The idea.** When two labels produce the same short name, generate distinct ones — a numeric suffix,
or a singular/plural heuristic — instead of stopping.
**Why it is attractive.** The build never breaks on a naming accident.
**The evidence against it.** The most mature implementation of derived names published the criterion
that condemns it: the algorithm must avoid anything that lets **a small change in the sample cause a
large change in the provided types**. Their own collision scheme was a numeric suffix, so a member's
name depended on which *other* members existed; their singulariser produced `Purchasis` and
`Sourcis`; and a minor version bump renamed members and broke a production application. The argument
does not have to be made from first principles — their criterion makes it.
**What would bring it back.** A real vocabulary where collisions are the norm rather than the
exception, at which point the short name has stopped being the name and the whole scheme needs
rethinking rather than patching.

### Resolving a prefix over the network at compile time

**The idea.** Look up an unknown namespace prefix in the public registry that exists for exactly
that.
**Why it is attractive.** The registry exists, it is well known, and it removes a declaration.
**The evidence against it.** That registry served an **expired certificate for seven months** while
every IRI it abbreviates continued to answer. IRIs are durable; the abbreviation layer over them
rots, and a compile that depends on it inherits its uptime.
**What would bring it back.** Nothing, at compile time. As an *editor* suggestion — offered,
ignorable, never load-bearing — it is a different thing and was never rejected.

## Shadows of inference from shapes

### Making SHACL the language the contract is written in

**The idea.** Take the checker's constraint table from SHACL rather than from ShEx.
**Why it is attractive.** SHACL is a W3C Recommendation with an active working group; ShEx's
standardisation moved elsewhere. On standards-body health alone it is the safer bet, and this is said
plainly because it is the cost of the choice.
**The evidence against it.** The axis being decided is not prestige, it is what the contract can
express. A contract that does not distinguish **"one"** from **"one or more"** is not the contract
being asked for, and closure has three states rather than two. The available SHACL path could not
carry either distinction, while the ShEx path already is a contract end to end: it types the input
side, it is read by the checker, and the same document describes the output.
**What would bring it back.** Three things, and none of them is an argument. **(1)** A client
arriving with a shape document that already exists and that nobody is going to rewrite. **(2)** SHACL
reaching Recommendation with the triple-term node kind in it **at the same time as** a real RDF 1.2
producer in the corpus — both, because the node kind without the producer checks nothing. **(3)** A
shape that needs a disjunction, where the current advice to split it into several mappings is going
to sound absurd the day the shape was written by somebody else.

### Deriving the shape document from the mapping

**The idea.** Generate the shape from the mapping, which is what most tooling in this space does.
**Why it is attractive.** It is free, it never disagrees with the mapping, and it produces a document
where there was none.
**The evidence against it.** It never disagrees with the mapping *because it was computed from it*.
The result is an after-the-fact description of whatever the mapping happened to do, typos included,
and a contract that cannot contradict you is not checking anything.
**What would bring it back.** A workflow in which nobody has a shape and the produced graph really is
the specification — where the generated document is documentation rather than a contract, and is
labelled as such.

## Shadows of the standard library

### Growing the catalogue ahead of the programs

**The idea.** Add the operation, the trait, the abstraction now, because it will obviously be wanted.
**Why it is attractive.** It is cheaper to design the general case once than to generalise later.
**The evidence against it.** One error, repeated at nine different scales in this project's own
history: **the abstraction was written before the second implementation existed** — extension points
with one implementation and no dispatch, operators defined and mostly unreachable, catalogue entries
that no program could call. The aggravating factor is what makes it worth recording: it was
**documented as finished**, so intent stopped being distinguishable from state, and the only way to
learn which was which was to read the code.
**What would bring it back.** The second implementation. That is the whole rule, and it is a
condition rather than a judgement.

### Plugins, as wasm modules or dynamic libraries

**The idea.** Let third parties add providers and functions without merging into the tree.
**Why it is attractive.** It is the standard answer to extensibility and it scales past the
maintainers.
**The evidence against it**, measured in three projects that did it: one editor maintains **ten
simultaneous ABI versions**; one database maintains a signed matrix of six platforms per release; one
compiler paid for openness with `Unknown` variants in every enum of its syntax tree, permanently. An
in-tree registration list delivers the entire benefit at zero ABI cost.
**What would bring it back.** A consumer who genuinely cannot merge into the tree. It is a door that
opens later, and opening it later costs nothing that opening it now would have saved.

### Moving the standard library into `catalogue.bnf`

**The idea.** The `io.` rows are a data file the compiler reads; the other fifty-one rows are the
same shape, so they belong in the same file. Adding `str.slugify` over `regexp_replace` would stop
touching Rust.
**Why it is attractive.** It is the consistent answer, `catalogue.bnf` names it as the shape the
stdlib would take, and the file already has a parser, five projections and a drift check waiting.
**The evidence against it.** Three measurements, none of them about tidiness. Swift's `.def` files
carried **1,364** rows with no generator at all before anyone reached for TableGen, and Clang left
`.def` at around **6,000**; the stdlib is **fifty-one**, two orders of magnitude below where a file
starts paying. `crates/xtask/src/reference.rs` already generates a page from the Rust table with no
`.bnf` in the path, which is the proof in the tree that generation — the thing the proof actually
comes from — does not need the file. And the one justification a data file has that a table does not
is a **second authoring audience**, somebody who must add a row without touching the Rust chain: it
is why `rust.ungram` exists, `catalogue.bnf` never claims one, and fossil has none.
**What would bring it back.** That second audience appearing. Nothing else — not row count on its
own, and not consistency with the `io.` half, which is in a file for a different reason: its
BEHAVIOUR half is linked by a host that may not have it.

### A uniform `HirSourceOp { op, args }` instead of typed variants

**The idea.** With every relation verb declaring its parameters, the lowered stage could be one
shape — the operator plus a vector of bound arguments — and the three typed variants could go.
**Why it is attractive.** Thirteen declared verbs against three constructible variants is an
asymmetry, and the uniform shape makes `lower_source_stage` end without a `match` at all.
**The evidence against it.** It trades a compile-time guarantee for a runtime unwrap: `args[0]`
expecting a predicate is checked by nobody, where `Where(HirExpr)` cannot hold a column list. It is
the same fork rustc and rust-analyzer took opposite ways over the same table — rustc's
`[Option<DefId>; N]` costs it a discriminant coupling, a `from_u32` its own comment calls *"clumsy"*
and a `panic!`-enforced bijection, while rust-analyzer's one typed `Option<TraitId>` field per row
has none of the three. fossil is on the IDE side of that line. The `match` that survives is
CONSTRUCTION rather than dispatch: three arms of three lines, exhaustive, with the unimplemented
verbs named.
**What would bring it back.** Enough lowered verbs that the typed variants stop being writable by
hand — the constructible set overtaking the declared one, which is the opposite of today's ten
declared and not lowered.

## Shadows of the surface

### Borrowing the comprehension binder

**The idea.** Write the source binding with the arrow that comprehensions use, as several functional
languages do.
**Why it is attractive.** It is instantly familiar and it reads as "drawn from".
**The evidence against it.** In the languages it would be borrowed from, the specification defines the
left side as an **element** and the right side as the **collection**. Here the left side names the
relation, so the borrowed glyph would say the opposite of what it means. It already means assignment
in one widely-read language and channel receive in another, and no language was found in which it
means "produced once per element of". It also flows right-to-left in a program whose pipelines flow
the other way.
**What would bring it back.** A form where the left-hand side really does denote one element rather
than the relation — an iteration construct, if one is ever needed.

### The anonymous row

**The idea.** Drop the receiver's name inside a mapping body and write bare `.field` against an
implicit current row.
**Why it is attractive.** It is the shortest possible spelling of the most common expression in the
language, and several query languages start there.
**The evidence against it** is a table with no counterexamples: every system that started with an
implicit receiver had to add a naming mechanism the moment two rows were in scope — a context
function, an explicit binding form, a variable binder, a pair of role keywords, a marker annotation,
named bindings replacing positional ones — and **not one went the other way.** Worse, the failure is
**silent and triggered by data rather than by code**: a path degrades quietly when the implicit
receiver is shadowed, and one language's implicit-scope construct is documented as having changed the
meaning of surrounding code because a record *gained a field*. In a language whose job is surviving
schema drift, that is the worst available failure mode.
**What would bring it back.** A source model with genuine hierarchy to descend, where the context
item earns its cost — the observation from that literature is that an implicit context item optimises
for hierarchical navigation rather than for joins, and tabular sources are all joins.

### Renaming silently when a join collides

**The idea.** When two joined relations share a column name, suffix them automatically, as the
mainstream data frame libraries do.
**Why it is attractive.** The join always succeeds and nobody has to think about it.
**The evidence against it.** It is the same failure mode as the anonymous row — triggered by the data,
invisible in the code — and the libraries that do it by default only raise when the suffixes are
turned off by hand.
**What would bring it back.** The other defensible option was never rejected on merit: flatten and
require qualification, which is what SQL does. It becomes the answer the day a row carrying two `id`
columns is what readers expect to see rather than something to be protected from.

### A join whose two keys have different names

**The idea.** Let the join condition relate two columns that are not spelled the same — the ordinary
`ON left.person_id = right.id`.
**Why it is attractive.** It is what every join in every query language does, and the restriction
below looks arbitrary from outside.
**The evidence against it.** Not evidence, a dependency: the condition is an equality with the key
named **once**, which is the same thing `USING (k)` is, and it is spelled that way because a
qualified condition needs both sides of the join addressable in one expression. That is a property
of the surface, not of the join. Taking the general form first would have meant designing the
qualified reference to satisfy a join rather than the language.
**What would bring it back**, and it is a condition rather than a judgement: **the first real mapping
whose two keys do not share a name.** Not an argument — a program somebody wants to write and
cannot. The qualified reference that makes it expressible is the same one everything else on the
surface needs, so this reopens by being unblocked rather than by being reconsidered.

### Letting a program omit its shape document

**The idea.** Let a program compile without naming a shape document, and check only what can be
checked from the source side.
**Why it is attractive.** It is the gentler on-ramp, and for a while it was the rule — a program with
no shape was explicitly *not an error*.
**The evidence against it.** It stopped being coherent the moment a property key became a bare name.
A bare key takes its name from a predicate that a shape **declares**, so without a shape there is no
name for a property to have — a program with no shape document cannot write a single property, and
what it produces is not a smaller check but an empty mapping. The failure mode is the bad one:
`TypeckOutput.predicates` is empty, every property fails to lower, and the program reports success
having written nothing.
**What would bring it back.** A second source of predicate names — a vocabulary the program names
directly rather than through a shape. That is a different feature, and until it exists the
permissive reading is not a relaxation, it is a program that silently produces nothing.

### The outer join

**The idea.** Support the other join variants, not only the inner one.
**Why it is attractive.** It is the ordinary set, and their absence looks like an omission.
**The evidence against it.** An outer join **fabricates nulls**, and a corpus whose contract asserts
that a property is present is a corpus into which a fabricated null must not be written. The
capability arrives with its nullability story or it does not arrive.
**What would bring it back**, written as a criterion rather than an opinion: *the first real mapping
whose keys do not share a name — not an argument, a program somebody wants to write and cannot.* The
criterion was met, and measuring it turned up something nobody had seen: under the algebra as
declared, two ordinary tables could not be joined **even with a qualified condition**, because they
share a primary-key column name and any shared name was an error. That was not a decision; it was an
unmeasured consequence prohibiting the join of any two tables with primary keys.

## Shadows of identity

### Identity as an argument to an attribute

**The idea.** Write the identity template inside an attribute above the mapping.
**Why it is attractive.** It is metadata, and attributes are where metadata goes.
**The evidence against it.** Seven languages, no exception: none admits a per-instance expression in
an attribute argument, and in one of them the specification calls the restriction a deliberate
syntactic device. The control experiment is the language that *does* evaluate expressions in
attributes — where the per-row form compiles, produces **one frozen value for every row**, and costs
its documentation a paragraph warning about a mistake other languages cannot express.
**What would bring it back.** The rule that decides it: *if the attribute can be omitted it is
forbidden, because omission implies a derived fallback; if it cannot be omitted it is not an
attribute but syntax with brackets, and the spelling is then irrelevant.* Make it mandatory in every
declaration and this stops being the rejected option and becomes a spelling question.

### Letting each mapping mint its own identity

**The idea.** Allow two mappings that produce one type to declare different identity templates.
**Why it is attractive.** It is what every mapping language does, and the one federation system that
checked it removed the check and published its reasons.
**The evidence against it.** The federation case does not transfer: its two keys are alternative ways
to **locate** one entity rather than two identities, and its subgraphs deploy separately so nobody
ever holds the whole set — the check here is available precisely because the topology is the
opposite. Meanwhile the same property is silently assumed by the academic work, detected but wired
only to an optimiser by the one engine that computes it, and guaranteed by construction in the one
W3C mapping that derives identity from the table's key rather than from a free template.
**What would bring it back**, in order. **(1)** A legitimate case of disagreement — disjoint
populations of one type with no common identity scheme; one real instance demotes the error to a
warning. **(2)** A topology where fossil composes mappings from **independent authorities**, at which
point the federation argument becomes ours. **(3)** Invertibility: virtual access over a live source
needs the inverse expression, and one template per type is not enough for it.

## Shadows of streaming, memory and scale

### Distributing the writer across machines

**The idea.** When one machine is not enough, use several.
**Why it is attractive.** It is the reflexive answer to "no ceiling".
**The evidence against it.** At the measured density, one machine reaches far past the scale that has
been measured, and the two mechanisms that extend it — memory mapping and spill — are not exhausted.
**Distributing before they are is trading a measured problem for a larger unmeasured one.**
**What would bring it back.** A corpus that exhausts one machine's **disk** rather than its memory.

### Tuning the constants inside the layout

**The idea.** Shrink the per-edge and per-vertex state — a tighter representation, a bitset instead of
a map.
**Why it is attractive.** The numbers are right there and the change is local.
**The evidence against it.** One such attempt regressed by seven gigabytes. And with *no ceiling* as
the goal, going from fifty-three bytes per edge to thirty does not change the category of the problem
— **it moves the point where it bursts.**
**What would bring it back.** A goal that is a ceiling rather than the absence of one: a fixed target
machine, where a constant factor is the difference between fitting and not.

### Adopting a community-detection library

**The idea.** Replace hand-written clustering with a maintained crate.
**Why it is attractive.** Nobody should hand-roll a graph algorithm, and one candidate is actively
developed.
**The evidence against it**, three independent reasons. **Functionality**: the hierarchy — every level
of it — is what earns this structure its place, and every candidate returns a *flat* partition, which
would leave the functions that consume it with nothing. **Scale**: the maintained ones are capped at
32-bit indices, and one candidate's own published table does not improve on what is already measured
here; swapping one resident implementation for another **moves the point where it bursts rather than
removing it**. **Re-measurement**: adopting means trading measured code carrying four separate
measurements for something whose memory profile on this corpus is unknown — and the failure mode is
already on record, since a correct rewrite in this exact area passed every parity test and regressed
seven gigabytes.
**What would bring it back**, named specifically: a layered-label-propagation implementation with
64-bit indices, a memory-mapped graph and spill to a working directory, written by the algorithm's
own author. Its costs are equally specific and are why it is a **measurement to schedule rather than a
dependency to add**: it is label propagation rather than modularity, so the compactness that
justified keeping the hierarchy has to be re-measured from zero; it requires a symmetric loopless
graph; it still needs roughly twenty-five bytes per node resident, which is twenty-five gigabytes at
a billion; and it wants a **second on-disk format**, which collides with "Parquet and nothing else".

### Adopting a space-filling-curve library

**The idea.** Take the interleaving from a crate instead of writing twenty-one lines.
**Why it is attractive.** It is the archetype of what should be a dependency.
**The evidence against it.** The most-downloaded candidate is a signature-for-signature copy of the
local implementation **capped at 16-bit two-dimensional coordinates** — which is exactly the ceiling
one would adopt it to escape — and it is the easy mistake precisely because it ranks first. And
adopting is not a speed play at all: the measured cost in this area is memory and network, not
arithmetic.
**What would bring it back**, stated exactly: the day the interleaving stops being a detail of the
writer and becomes **the contract between two repositories** — when a reader has to compute the same
address the writer wrote, and a shared, third-party-tested implementation is worth more than twenty-one
lines on each side. The well-made candidate for that day is generic in the number of dimensions with
the output width as an associated type, which is the shape a flat identifier would need.

### Adopting a layout library

**The idea.** Use an existing graph-layout crate.
**Why it is attractive.** Layout is a solved problem with decades of literature.
**The evidence against it.** There is nothing to adopt, and that *is* the finding: no crate implements
the placement used here, there is no port of the standard multilevel force layouts, and **no layout
crate publishes a benchmark above ten thousand nodes**. There is also a hard licence ceiling
independent of any technical merit — the best-known force implementation is licensed incompatibly
with this workspace, and the only compatible one has a lifetime download count in the dozens.
**What would bring it back.** A maintained implementation with a compatible licence publishing a
benchmark at the size where this work *starts*.

<Callout title="The thread through the last three">
In all three cases **the ecosystem ends almost exactly where these sizes begin.** No layout crate
benchmarks above ten thousand nodes; no maintained community-detection crate exceeds 32-bit indices;
the most popular curve crate stops at 16-bit coordinates. The small corpus here is ten million. That
is not a preference for local code — it is that the frontier of what can be bought falls below the
starting point, which is why what *is* bought is always small and verifiable and never large.
</Callout>

### Replacing the curve with a better-behaved one

**The idea.** Use a curve with better locality than the one in use — a well-known and genuinely
superior ordering in the general case.
**Why it is attractive.** It is the textbook improvement, and the best-maintained crate in the whole
search implements it.
**The evidence against it.** Measured on this corpus, the hypothesis is **false**: the improvement did
not appear. Adopting anyway would be replacing measured code with code to be measured, against a
hypothesis already refuted here.
**What would bring it back.** A corpus with a different shape, measured — the refutation is about this
data, not about the curves.

### Unifying the two execution engines

**The idea.** One engine everywhere instead of two.
**Why it is attractive.** Two engines mean two SQL dialects, two configuration surfaces and two sets
of behaviour to keep in step.
**The evidence against it.** The margin does not pay: the SQL is a small fraction of the slice it sits
in, and unifying would mean rewriting roughly nine hundred lines of query construction as plan
construction, adding several megabytes to the read path, and **losing the range-request file system
that makes reading a corpus over HTTP work at all**.
**What would bring it back.** The read path no longer needing range requests over plain HTTP — which
would be a different product.

## Shadows of the corpus

### Conforming to an existing graph interchange specification

**The idea.** Emit an established columnar graph format instead of a bespoke layout.
**Why it is attractive.** Interoperability, for free, with a specification somebody else maintains.
**The evidence against it.** It **gave vocabulary and stopped giving contract**: its specification
defines no count and no data-file path, so derivability had to be invented anyway — and two questions
that were declared blocking existed *only* because of conformance. As an
interchange format it does not pay either, since a recipient would have to learn an incubating
specification, where self-explaining file names are more interoperable than a specification nobody
has read. And conformance is not something that was given up in principle and kept in practice:
every vertex manifest fossil writes declares `dense_id` as `uint32` with `is_primary: true`
(`crates/fossil-df/src/lib.rs:1798`), and the built-in list GraphAr's `types.cc` parses has no
unsigned type at all — so its own C++ reader would refuse the primary key of the first vertex type.
What survives as an internal invariant is the good part: **the manifest's field names**, both
adjacency orientations, dense identifiers, and edges aligned to the source's chunk. The full
enumeration — eight divergences, each one the same rule applied once — is in [what is borrowed from
GraphAr, and where borrowing
stops](/docs/design/corpus#what-is-borrowed-from-graphar-and-where-borrowing-stops).
**What would bring it back.** A second reader that is not ours, that already speaks it.

### One file per tile

**The idea.** Give every tile its own file, so a request is a file.
**Why it is attractive.** The addressing is trivial and every request is exactly one object.
**The evidence against it.** Measured against the alternative: **four times the requests and not one
byte saved.** A request against a single file is a maximal run of consecutive row groups, and the
tiles a window needs merge into a handful of runs — the file boundary is the only thing that prevents
the merge.
**What would bring it back.** A store where opening a file is cheaper than a range request into a
large one.

### A materialised tile tree, holding the edges that cross tiles

**The idea.** A pyramid of tiles, with each edge stored at the deepest tile containing both of its
endpoints.
**Why it is attractive.** It is the classical answer, it removes the cross-tile case, and interior
nodes look like the natural home for it.
**The evidence against it.** An interior node earns its existence only by **containing** something a
leaf cannot, and the only candidate is the crossing edge — which is measured to cost more to lift
than it saves. The reads diverge sharply: one placement stays flat as the corpus grows fifty-fold and
the other grows with it, and it loses on request count at the same time. The structural cause is
that near the root there is **no branching left to prune with**, so every window touches nearly every
high tile — and every window pays the root whether or not anything in it is drawn, because a reader
cannot know what a tile holds without reading it. That is what addressing *means*. With nothing to
put in them, the interior nodes are empty, and the addressing that replaces the tree is arithmetic on
identifiers.
**What would bring it back.** Nothing declared — but the hole in the measurement is named: an edge
with one endpoint off-screen has a visible segment and **neither placement brings it**. Measuring
that needs the other adjacency orientation, and it duplicates the addressing rather than the
argument.

### Capability negotiation for the artifact

**The idea.** A monotonic version plus an unordered set of named capabilities a reader must
understand, split into read and write.
**Why it is attractive.** It is the mechanism the mature table formats have, and retrofitting it is
expensive.
**The evidence against it.** *There is no problem to solve.* Nothing is published and there are no
older readers, so a negotiation mechanism answers a question nobody has asked — and then has to be
maintained until somebody asks it.
**What would bring it back**, written so nobody has to remember it: **the day a corpus leaves this
machine towards somebody who cannot regenerate it.** That is the same boundary as publishing the
arithmetic test vectors, for the same reason — the enduring cost in this space is files that can no
longer be withdrawn.

### A second writer

Not a rejection but a standing reversal condition, recorded because it flips a decision rather than
adjusting one. **If a second writer appears — anybody producing this corpus without using this
implementation — the priority inverts**: the specification becomes the contract and the
implementation becomes one implementation among several. *One writer is what makes the library half
safe; two writers is what turned a widely-used columnar format into a reference implementation that
became normative by accident.* And the clock runs one way: if the corpus reaches third parties before
the arithmetic is published, the option is gone.

## Shadows of the tooling

### Removing the incremental core from the batch path

**The idea.** The batch path builds an empty dependency database on every invocation and never gets a
cache hit across runs, so remove the machinery and take the saving.
**Why it is attractive.** The diagnosis is correct — there really is no reuse between invocations —
and a borrowed figure from a comparable project reported a 52% improvement from exactly this change.
**The evidence against it.** The borrowed figure does not transfer, and measuring locally says why:
that project is a **library whose entire work is compilation**, so its percentage of compilation *is*
its percentage of the process. Here compilation is two thousandths of a command dominated by process
start-up and opening a database connection. The ceiling on the saving is **under four percent of that
two thousandths, falling to under half a percent as the program grows** — and the same measurement
found the real cost, which is quadratic behaviour in one lowering step, entirely unrelated. Two
structural blocks came with it: the memoised core is the type through which the whole internal API
passes, so replacing it is a rewrite of thousands of signatures; and the browser artifact reaches it
through the executor regardless of whether the database is built.
**What would bring it back**, and half of it is already measured against: *a profile where compilation
stops being microseconds — a file with hundreds of mappings, or a check called in a loop inside a
long-lived process.* The first of those is measured and points the wrong way. What is **not** measured
is the cost in bytes of the browser artifact, which would be a different argument from the one about
time.

### A subprocess as the embedding surface

**The idea.** A product embeds the language by spawning the binary and reading JSON off its stdout.
Each capability it needs becomes a verb with a machine-readable flag.
**Why it is attractive.** It is the cheapest possible integration — no linking, no version skew in the
build, no wasm toolchain — and it works on day one for any host that can start a process.
**The evidence against it.** It makes the *binary* the API, and the binary is the one host that cannot
be embedded where the product actually runs. Measured on 2026-08-16: the product that this surface was
built for spawns no fossil binary anywhere, and both of its images declare that they contain none —
`run`, `refs`, `providers` and `check` all execute in the browser. The verbs and their wire types
outlived their only consumer, and while they lived a second implementation of each grew beside them,
which is how the serializer divergence on this page's parent got in. The subprocess is not wrong as a
*transport*; it is wrong as the definition of what fossil offers.
**What would bring it back.** A host that cannot run wasm at all, or a corpus whose run genuinely
needs facilities `wasm32` does not have — a disk to spill to being the concrete one, since the
compiler layer's promise of no ceiling is stated on this page's parent as living only where there is a
disk. Neither is a reason to keep a second copy of a capability the browser already performs; both are
reasons for the command line to remain a client.

### A linked Rust type as the contract between fossil and a host

**The idea.** The host depends on a small, dependency-light, wasm-clean crate holding the wire structs,
so both sides deserialize the same Rust definition and the contract cannot skew.
**Why it is attractive.** It is genuinely one source of truth, it is checked by the compiler on both
sides, and it costs the host a single lightweight dependency.
**The evidence against it.** The crate accumulates contracts that have nothing to do with each other —
the run result, the provider registry and the typed lineage arrived in one crate named for the first
of them — and it accumulates build machinery belonging to the consumer, which is how an optional
schema-derive feature whose own comment read "Host-only" came to live in a compiler crate. Measured:
of the three contracts, only the run result is referenced from the host's Rust at all; the other two
are consumed from TypeScript through the wasm package, so the linked crate is carrying two contracts
for a language that never links it. A linked type also pins the host to a tag: dropping that feature
means the host cannot bump past its pinned tag until it stops asking for it.
**What would bring it back.** A host in the same workspace, or one that wants the compiler to enforce
the contract at *its* build time rather than at its deserialize time — which is a real thing to want,
and is a different argument from the one that put three unrelated contracts in one crate.

### An unmaintained language-server framework

**The idea.** Use the ergonomic framework rather than the low-level protocol library.
**Why it is attractive.** It is the obvious choice by search ranking and by API pleasantness.
**The evidence against it.** Roughly three years without maintenance, and all three reference
implementations in this space use the low-level library instead.
**What would bring it back.** Maintenance resuming — and until then the ban is explicit rather than
tacit, so reintroducing it takes a decision rather than an import.

### An editor client that consumes semantic tokens

**The idea.** Adopt an existing browser editor language-client that handles semantic highlighting.
**Why it is attractive.** It is exactly the missing piece and two candidates advertise the area.
**The evidence against it**, obtained by probing the packages rather than reading their prose: of
twenty-seven exports and seventeen prototype methods in the leading candidate, **none** matches
semantic tokens; the other candidate's semantic-sounding strings are initialisation options passed
through to the server, not consumers of a response. The generic request escape hatch does exist and
does return the payload.
**What would bring it back.** A client that consumes the response rather than forwarding an option —
and the transferable part is the technique: probe a package by its exports and its prototype before
adopting it.
