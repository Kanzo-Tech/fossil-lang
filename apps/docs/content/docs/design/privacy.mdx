A fossil corpus is files, handed over. The recipient reads every column with any Parquet reader.
**That is not fixable, and this page is what follows from accepting it.**

## The consequence, taken rather than fought

There is no chokepoint. No process of ours stands between the files and whoever has them, so **no
read-time privacy control is possible** — not a masked column, not a row filter, not a permission on
a verb.

The temptation is to answer this by withholding the escape hatch: gate `execute_sql` and the problem
looks smaller. It is not smaller, it is hidden. [`read`'s `where` is a raw SQL
fragment](/docs/design/corpus#reads-where-is-sql-and-carries-execute_sqls-authority) evaluated by
the same engine, so gating one and not the other is the same surface with a second door — and even
with both gated, the recipient has the Parquet.

The systems that genuinely do this have a chokepoint and still do not put the control on SQL.
Snowflake applies dynamic data masking and row access policies inside the query planner; BigQuery
does column-level ACLs and row-level security the same way; Databricks likewise. The control is
**below** the query language, which is exactly the position a file format does not have.

So:

- Protection is **total at write time**. The bytes that would violate the bound are never written,
  and every reader preserves it for free.
- What travels with the artifact is a **declaration plus a verifiable property**, never an
  enforcement. [The declared bound](/docs/format/conventions/privacy) is the format.

<Callout type="warn" title="Two products, and only one of them is this">
A corpus that is **served** — the operator keeps the files, access is only through their engine —
does have a chokepoint, and it admits what this cannot: query-time perturbation, differential
privacy, a budget that runs out. It also costs what this does not: a trusted operator, and an answer
that degrades with use.

That is a different product and nothing here is built for it. Nothing here assumes it away either:
the manifest states what the *bytes* guarantee, and a served deployment is free to guarantee more on
top.
</Callout>

## The language names no anonymisation operator

There is no `anon.generalise`, no `anon.mask`, no `anon.hash`. The reason is not that they are hard.

**An obligation an author discharges by remembering to call a function is one they can forget**, and
the failure is silent in the worst possible way: the program compiles, the corpus writes, the
manifest seals, and the missing call is discovered by the recipient. Nothing in the pipeline is in a
position to notice, because a mapping that does not call a function looks exactly like a mapping
that had no need to.

The bound is stated instead, and the writer refuses. The author's obligation becomes "reach k",
which is checkable, rather than "remember to anonymise", which is not.

## The classification is a separate document, and not an annotation on the shape

A program already binds one external document per output type — `type { Person } :=
io.shex("persons.shex")` — and [shapes](/docs/design/shapes) is where that stands. The obvious
place for a privacy classification is inside it: ShEx and SHACL both have annotation mechanisms, and
the shape already enumerates every predicate.

**It must not go there, and the reason is reuse.** A shape says what a `Person` *is*: which
predicates it carries, with what cardinality, at what datatype. That is a property of the
vocabulary, and it holds in every jurisdiction and every deployment. Whether `birth_year` is a
quasi-identifier is none of those things — it depends on what else is released, on who the recipient
is, and on which regulator is reading.

Putting the second inside the first produces one shape per deployment, differing only in
annotations. That is how a vocabulary stops being shared.

So it is a second document, bound the same way, and the two are joined by **the predicate IRI they
both name**.

## Three vocabularies, and the gaps that forced a fourth

| | what it supplies | where it stops |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ODRL 2.2** | The structure: `Policy`, `Permission`, `Prohibition`, `Constraint`, and the `leftOperand`/`operator`/`rightOperand` triple | `odrl:target` names an `Asset`. There is **no granularity below it** — no column, no attribute, no field, no schema |
| **DPV 2.3** | Classification values: `dpv:IdentifyingPersonalData`, `dpv:SensitivePersonalData` | It names privacy *techniques* and **cannot parameterise them**, and it has no quasi-identifier at all |
| **ISO/IEC 20889:2018** | The definitions the terms stand for | It is a paywalled standard, not a vocabulary — nothing dereferences |

The two gaps are load-bearing and both were checked rather than assumed.

**DPV cannot hold a number.** Not one `xsd:decimal`, `xsd:integer`, `xsd:float` or `xsd:double`
appears in DPV core, `dpv-tech` or `dpv-risk`, and every `has*` property is concept-valued —
`dpv:hasScale`'s range is `dpv:Scale`, an enumeration, not a magnitude. `dpv:DifferentialPrivacy`
exists as a bare class you assert or do not; **there is nowhere in DPV to put an ε or a k.**

**DPV has no quasi-identifier.** Case-insensitive search across all eight DPV 2.3 serializations for
`QuasiIdentifier`, `IndirectIdentifier` or `DirectIdentifier` returns nothing. The combination case
— which is the whole of k-anonymity — is absent.

### The profile is five terms

ODRL sanctions the extension in as many words. Information Model §3.3: *"Additional Constraint left
operands: Create an instance of the LeftOperand class."* And §3.2 buys the property worth having:
*"if the ODRL Processing system does not recognise the ODRL Profile identifier(s) then it MUST stop
processing the policy."* A runtime that does not know this profile is **required to stop, not to
guess**.

| term | why it is not somebody else's |
| --- | --- |
| `fossil:attribute` | ODRL has no sub-asset granularity. This is the term that does most of the work |
| `fossil:classification` | The dimension a `Constraint`'s single triple cannot carry alone |
| `fossil:anonymityK` | DPV has no numeric range |
| `fossil:absentQuasiIdentifier` | Likewise, and it is the parameter the whole check turns on |
| `fossil:suppressionBudget` | Likewise |

Plus two `skos:Concept` values — `fossil:QuasiIdentifier` and `fossil:NoDisclosureRisk` — where DPV
has nothing to borrow. The classification values that DPV *does* have are DPV's.

**Five is the ceiling and it is a test.** Catena-X is the one production European data-space profile
and it ships **four** `odrl:LeftOperand`s — `FrameworkAgreement`, `Membership`, `ContractReference`,
`UsagePurpose` — for an entire automotive industry, and not one of them is schema-aware. A sixth
term here needs an argument.

<Callout type="warn" title="The interoperability claim is narrower than it is usually made">
The reason normally given for minting into ODRL is that the Dataspace Protocol leaves `leftOperand`
open. **It does not.** DSP's `contract-schema.json` pins `LeftOperand` to a closed `enum` of exactly
the 34 ODRL Core instances — no extension slot, no `pattern`, no `anyOf` — so a policy carrying
`fossil:attribute` fails that schema.

What is true is narrower and still worth having. DSP's SHACL shapes constrain `odrl:target`,
`dspace:timestamp`, `odrl:assigner` and `odrl:assignee` and place **no** constraint on
`leftOperand`; its prose says nothing about it either. The closed enum is one artefact of three, and
the same file carries `odrl:term-lteq` in its `Operator` enum — an HTML anchor pasted where the term
IRI goes — which is a fair indication of how much load it is bearing. Eclipse EDC shipped the enum
as a Java type, had it filed as *"a severe blocker"* because the model *"cannot express constraints
other than those defined in the enum class"*, and moved to free-form.

So: an ODRL profile in ODRL's own sense, readable by anything that reads ODRL, and rejected by a
**strict** DSP validator until DSP's schema catches up with ODRL's extension point. That is a defect
in one JSON Schema, not a reason to invent a fifth format.
</Callout>

## Verification is the part that is easy to get wrong

The check is not `GROUP BY qi HAVING count(*) < k`. [The declared
bound](/docs/format/conventions/privacy#three-ways-to-compute-k-and-a-group-by-picks-one-by-accident)
specifies the three traps and what the manifest publishes about each; the short version is that
nulls have two defensible readings that disagree on the same file, that a suppression limit is part
of what k-anonymity means in practice, and that a corpus of tiles makes a per-tile aggregation the
easiest wrong answer available.

The verifier **measures and refuses**. It derives nothing: no generalisation search, no recoding, no
rows dropped and no cells blanked. A verifier that also repairs is a verifier whose failures nobody
ever sees — and the repair belongs upstream, where the author can see what it cost.

Its refusals name counts, column names and type labels, and **never a cell**. A diagnostic about a
privacy violation that prints the violating row has moved the disclosure out of the artifact and
into the log, the terminal scrollback and whatever CI captured — none of which was ever going to be
checked by a guard.

## What is claimed, and what is merely declared

The honest split, because the two are easy to blur:

**Checked, by anyone, from the files:** that the classes over the declared quasi-identifiers reach
the declared `k`; that the aggregation spanned the release; that the suppression is inside the
budget; that the manifest's numbers are the bytes' numbers.

**Declared, and checkable by nobody:** that the quasi-identifier set is the right one; that
`subject` is opaque; that anything a producer chose not to write was ever there. Those are stated on
the guard as what it cannot prove, because a guard that overstates its reach is worse than a missing
one.
