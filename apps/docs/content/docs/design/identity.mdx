Every node in an RDF graph is an IRI, and an IRI is not in your CSV. Somebody has to mint it. Where
that instruction lives is the single hardest question in the language's surface, and it is the one
place where the two documents a program is written against — the source and the shape — are both
silent.

## The shape document cannot declare it

A shape document describes the predicates of a node. In RDF the subject **is** the node, not one of
its properties, so there is no slot in the document to assign an identity to. That is not an
oversight in ShEx or in SHACL, and it is not fixed by choosing the other one.

The comparison that makes it clear is with the schema languages that *do* declare identity —
`identifier: true` in LinkML, `@id` in Prisma, `@key` in GraphQL. All three describe property
graphs, and in a property graph identity is **signalled**: one of the fields you already have is the
one that identifies. In RDF identity is **constructed**: the identifier is an IRI that exists
nowhere in the source and has to be built out of the row.

So the instruction has to live in the program, and the question is what shape it takes there.

## Not an attribute argument, and the prior art is unanimous

The attractive form is an attribute — `@subject(iri = "…/{User.email}")` — because an attribute is
where metadata goes and this is metadata. It does not survive contact with the prior art. Seven
languages were examined and there is **not one exception**:

- **Java** does not merely lack expressions in annotation arguments; the specification says it is a
  deliberate device. JLS §9.7.1 calls the restriction *"a syntactic trick to prevent assignment
  expressions as element values"*.
- **JPA**'s `@Id` is literally empty — `public @interface Id {}` — and expresses the identity **by
  where it is written**, not by what it carries.
- **Prisma** has no expression language at all in this position: nine function names, closed,
  implemented in the engine.
- **Ecto is the control experiment, and it is the most valuable item in the set.** Elixir *does*
  evaluate expressions in module attributes, so the per-row form compiles — and is wrong.
  `default: DateTime.utc_now` produces **one frozen value for every row**, and Ecto spends a
  paragraph of documentation warning about a mistake that in Java cannot be written. Given the full
  power, it ended up routing per-row generation through an indirection of inert data anyway.

The prior art also prescribes the exit when a per-row expression is genuinely needed, and it is
never *widen the attribute slot*: it is **give the thing its own named construct** — SQL's
`GENERATED ALWAYS AS`, Django's `GeneratedField`. And the failure mode to avoid has a name too:
Spring's `@PreAuthorize("…")`, where the expression comes back as an unchecked string and every
guarantee the surrounding type system offered is lost at the quotation mark.

That yields a rule sharp enough to apply mechanically:

> **If the attribute can be omitted, it is forbidden** — because an omissible attribute implies a
> derived fallback, and a derived identity is one nobody wrote down. **If it cannot be omitted, it is
> not an attribute; it is syntax with brackets**, and then the spelling does not matter.

RDFBeans demonstrates the second half by accident: its annotations are *required*, and without them
the class is not recognised at all. That is a grammar wearing an annotation's clothes.

## The slot, and why `@`

fossil takes the prescribed exit. Identity is a slot the language owns, written as an assignment, on
the first line of a mapping body:

<Program src="shop/shop.fossil" region="identity" title="shop.fossil" />

It is an ordinary assignment and its right-hand side is the ordinary expression grammar — the same
expressions, the same checking, the same completion. There is no second mini-language to keep in
sync with the first, which is the whole reason the string-in-an-attribute form is dangerous.

The `@` marks the **language's own namespace**, against the bare names a shape document declares.
That is not decoration. Property keys are the last segment of a predicate IRI, and a vocabulary is
free to declare a predicate called `subject`; without a reserved marker, the language and the user's
vocabulary compete for the same word. JSON-LD reserves `@`-prefixed keywords for exactly this
reason, and states it exactly this way: the user's keys are arbitrary, so you cannot appropriate
`id`. Django gives the criterion for what belongs in that space — *model metadata is anything that
is not a field*.

The mechanism generalises rather than being a one-off. A second language-owned slot — the named
graph a triple belongs to, for instance — enters through the same door instead of opening a second
one.

(`#` was the other candidate for the marker and is disqualified three times over: it is the comment
character in both Turtle and SPARQL, the two languages a fossil reader already has in their head; it
is the fragment separator in half the RDF vocabularies in existence; and a bare `#` is not lexable in
this grammar at all.)

## Two obligations no other property has

Because an IRI *denotes* — because writing one is a claim that this node is that thing — the identity
slot carries two rules that no ordinary property carries.

**It is deterministic and bounded to the row.** An identity that depends on anything that is not the
data is not reproducible, and a corpus you cannot regenerate is not a corpus. This is a check, not a
style guide.

**Every mapping that produces a type writes the same one.** Two mappings producing `Person` with
different identity templates is an **error**, and the diagnostic names both mappings and both
templates:

<Program src="errors/two-identities/expected/diagnostic.txt" title="fossil check program.fossil" />

Error rather than warning, and the reason is what happens when the warning is ignored: two entities
in the graph where there was one, discovered by whoever queries it. A warning is an appropriate
response to something the user might have meant. Nobody means this.

## The uniqueness check has no precedent, and one live counterexample

**Nothing in the mapping world checks this.** Not R2RML, not RML, not their mappers, not the mapping
quality literature; the community closed the request as out of scope. One system has the detector and
does not use it as one: Ontop derives incompatible-template information and wires it **only to the
optimiser**, as a way to prune a branch. And the academic work simply assumes the property — Xiao et
al. (ESWC 2018) write *"it is desired that each IRI can be constructed by at most one IRI template,
and we make such assumption here"*.

The W3C provides the contrast inside its own house. **Direct Mapping** derives identity from the
table and its primary key, so agreement holds **by construction** and there is nothing to check.
**R2RML §9.1.1** opened the free per-mapping template — and that is precisely where the problem is
born, unchecked, and has stayed.

**The counterexample that has to be answered rather than ignored: Apollo Federation shipped this
check and then removed it.** `KEY_NOT_SPECIFIED` existed in v1; v2 deleted it and published the
reason — *"Each subgraph can declare a key independently of any other subgraph"*. Verified live: two
subgraphs declaring disjoint keys compose with **"COMPOSED OK — no errors, no hints"**, while a
merely divergent documentation comment does raise a hint.

Two things make it not apply.

**Their two keys are two ways to *locate* one entity**, not two identities. They are alternative
keys, like two `UNIQUE` constraints on a table — the entity is one, and either key finds it. What
fossil rejects is two mappings *minting* different identifiers, which is not an alternative key but a
contradiction.

**And Apollo could not check it even if it wanted to.** Its subgraphs deploy separately; nobody ever
has the whole set in front of them at one moment. fossil is write-once/read-many and compiles a whole
program at once, so **the global check is available precisely because the topology is the opposite.**
The removal is correct there for a reason that argues *for* the check here.

## The rigidity, paid

Two sources describing **disjoint populations of the same type with different identity schemes** stop
being expressible. A legacy export keyed by account number and a current export keyed by email cannot
both produce `Person` in one program without agreeing on how a `Person` is named.

That is a real restriction and it is accepted knowingly, not overlooked.

There is also a piece of guidance from the Linked Data literature that plays against this design, and
it should be said rather than omitted: *"Agents making use of URIs SHOULD NOT attempt to infer
properties of the referenced resource"* — and the unit of minting policy in that literature is the
**publisher**, not the type. fossil chooses the type because the compiler is the single authority
over a program, which is an argument from topology rather than from that literature.

## What would reverse this, in order

1. **A legitimate case of disagreement.** Two mappings of one type that genuinely *must* mint
   differently — disjoint populations with no common identity scheme. **One real instance demotes the
   check from error to warning.**
2. **The distinction with Federation failing to hold** — that is, a topology in which fossil composes
   mappings from **independent authorities**. There, their argument becomes ours.
3. **Invertibility.** If virtual access over a live source is ever wanted, one template per type is
   not enough; that use needs the inverse expression, and the design would have to grow a place to
   put it.

And two things that were **not** verified, said rather than glossed: two R2RML mapping-quality
taxonomies sit behind paywalls and are the most likely place a named category for "inconsistent IRI
template" already exists; and **no published study measures how often a real mapping set mints two
IRIs for one entity.** The damage is documented in teaching material, not in a failure report.
