## Six, and why six

`fossil-graph` defines the surface; MCP, HTTP, the CLI and the in-process TS package are bindings
over it. The destination is six verbs:

| verb | what it answers |
| --- | --- |
| `read` | rows from a relation, with an optional `where:` |
| `expand{into\|all}` | the neighbourhood of a set of vertices, one hop |
| `path` | a route between two vertices |
| `aggregate` | a grouped, bounded summary |
| `schema` | what types, fields and relations exist |
| `execute_sql` | the escape hatch, which a binding may hide behind a permission |

Six is not a tidiness target. It follows from a decision about what the surface is *for*: the
algebra answers questions and **does not draw**. Drawing is addressing — the camera works out which
level and which tiles it needs and asks for bytes, with no bbox in flight, no SQL and no database on
that path. Level-of-detail is not a filter but a different relation: a level-3 tile holds super-nodes
that do not exist at level 0. A `where` selects rows from a table; zoom changes the table.

Once that is settled, every verb whose job was to make a picture stops having a job.

<Callout title="The rule that keeps it at six">
A new verb enters only if it serves a case the single path demonstrably cannot, and that
demonstration is a measurement. Not an argument.
</Callout>

## The mapping

The surface has already moved once toward this. It was seventeen; four of those were declared,
carried params and result types and wire schemas, and were never implemented — dispatch fell through
to a `NotImplemented` arm. They were deleted rather than written, which took the surface to thirteen
and took `GraphError::NotImplemented` with it: the `match` is now exhaustive, so a verb can no longer
be added without being wired. The compiler says what a stub used to say at runtime.

### Four schema verbs collapse to `schema`

`list_vertex_types`, `list_edge_types`, `describe_field`, `describe_vertex_type` → **`schema`**.

All four are manifest reads with no SQL behind them. They differ in what they project, which is an
argument, not a verb.

### Three aggregation verbs collapse to `aggregate`

`aggregate`, `histogram`, `top_k` → **`aggregate`**.

A histogram is a group-by on a bucketed expression; a top-k is a group-by with an order and a limit.
Both were spelled as separate verbs because they had separate result shapes, and separate result
shapes is what a parameterised verb is for.

### Three keep their meaning and change their name

| today | destination |
| --- | --- |
| `get_vertex` | `read(where:)` — one vertex is a predicate, not a verb |
| `find_neighbors` | `expand` |
| `find_path` | `path` |

`execute_sql` survives unchanged, and it survives on purpose: an arbitrary crossfilter can only
filter what is resident, so a general question needs a general escape hatch. Mapbox has the same
limit and does not solve it either.

### Four disappeared without ever being implemented

`search_by_label`, `summarize_cluster`, `answer_with_communities`, `set_selection`.

Three of the four were never verbs. `summarize_cluster` and `answer_with_communities` are a `read`
over a hierarchy level. `set_selection` always belonged to the client — a selection is state the
canvas holds, not a question the corpus answers. `search_by_label` needed an embedding column that no
writer emits, so it was gated on work that had not happened.

Deleting these was free in the strict sense: no implementation, no caller. The interesting part is
what the deletion bought, which is the exhaustive `match` above.

### Two disappear when tiles exist, and not one commit before

`viewport` and `materialize_graph`.

These are the two the destination has no room for and today's code cannot do without. `viewport` is
the only verb that draws: bbox in, vertices and edges out, with a detail mode and an aggregate mode
selected by an `if` inside the verb — which is relation selection wearing a predicate's clothes,
and precisely the confusion the tile design removes.

<Callout type="warn" title="They are load-bearing until the tiles land">
There is no tile store, no tile index and no tile emission yet. Until there is, `viewport` is how
the graph read path is expressed: it is dispatched over MCP, it is a method on the
`@fossil-lang/graph` client, and the two SQL statements it emits are what the scale benchmarks
actually execute. Removing it before the replacement exists deletes the read path and replaces it
with nothing.

One correction to a claim that circulates about this: **no viewer in this repository reads through
`viewport`.** `packages/viewer` does not import `@fossil-lang/graph` at all. The verb's consumers are
the MCP binding, the TS client's method surface, and the tests.
</Callout>

## The trap ADR-0042 records about `expand{into}`

`expand{into}` is on the destination list, and the reasoning that put it there does not hold on our
engine. It should be read with that attached.

The justification came from Kùzu and Neo4j, which push a mask of candidate destinations *into* the
scan so the expansion never materialises neighbours it will discard. On DuckDB that class of pruning
is measured and unavailable. A window of 20,000 vertices at five million is 179 contiguous runs of
`dense_id` covering 20,007 ids — a 268× superset of the visible edges with zero over-read, and
**no way of asking for it in SQL wins**:

| how the prune was expressed | time |
| --- | --- |
| no pruning at all (plain join) | 5 ms |
| range join against the runs | 237 ms |
| 179 `BETWEEN … OR …` predicates | 189 ms, 2.3 s CPU |

Both attempts are slower than not pruning, because the engine evaluates the ranges per row instead of
skipping row groups.

**The verb is right as a shape. Its performance motivation is not available to us.** That is why the
same measurement produced the addressing decision: pruning cannot be a predicate, only a choice of
what to read.
