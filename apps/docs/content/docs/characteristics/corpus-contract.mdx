## Why a corpus needs a contract at all

A corpus is bytes on disk that outlive the process that wrote them, and it is read by code that was
not compiled against the code that wrote it. Dense ids, the Morton order, the CSR/CSC pair, the chunk
addressing — every one of those is arithmetic that a reader has to re-derive, and re-derivation is
where formats go wrong.

The decision about *how* that arithmetic ships was taken on the evidence of nine formats: Arrow,
Parquet, Iceberg, Delta, MVT, PMTiles, Zarr, GraphAr, and H3 against S2.

## The destination: four obligations, none optional

<Callout title="Decided, and partly built">
All four are one decision, argued from those nine formats in [the corpus](/docs/design/corpus). Some
of each is on disk and none of them is finished;
[what is absent, re-measured](#what-is-absent-re-measured) says which part, obligation by obligation,
and it is the section to trust wherever this one reads like a promise.
</Callout>

### 1. The arithmetic publishes as Iceberg publishes it, not as PMTiles does

Iceberg specifies its bucket transform as Murmur3 x86 32-bit with seed 0 — the formula, the widths,
the sign — **and a table of vectors** by type, of which `34 → 2017239379` is one. Searching for that
constant finds it in the tests of independent implementations that copied it. PMTiles dispatches its
Hilbert curve with a link to Wikipedia, and every port re-derives it.

So `tile = dense_id >> 12` gets written with the width of the shift, the width of the integer, the
sign, and a table of vectors at the boundaries: 0, 4,095, 4,096, 2³¹−1, 2³¹, 2⁵³. **The vectors are
the deliverable**, because the vectors are what gets copied. They ship inside the corpus as Parquet,
so every layer reads them with what it already has.

### 2. 64-bit ids are `BigInt` at the TypeScript boundary, and `number` is a lint error

This is not precaution. `node-s2` was a **binding** — not a port, not a reimplementation — and it
returned wrong cell ids anyway, because JavaScript's `number` carries 53 bits and a cell id carries
64. **A binding does not protect a language boundary that cannot hold the value.**

H3 settled it by decree, typing `H3Index` as a string before anyone could get it wrong. We settle it
the same way: `BigInt` at the boundary, `number` forbidden for an id by lint rule. `>>` means three
different things across our three layers, and in JavaScript it coerces to 32 bits. What goes to the
GPU stays a typed array; what *identifies* does not.

### 3. Our TypeScript reader calls the same compiled Rust

Not a reimplementation. That is the H3 model, and it costs nothing here because the writer is already
Rust. H3 wins its comparison with S2 not because bindings are magic but because it has **one oracle
and almost nobody reimplements it** — zero divergence incidents across its ecosystem. S2 has four
oracles in a chain (`rust-s2` is a port of the Go one, `s2js` is "compatible with" the Go one: ports
of a port) and no shared vectors, with concrete divergences open for years.

Third parties — measured, they exist — get the specification and the vectors, which is the half a
wasm module cannot give them.

### 4. Conformance is round-trip, and it blocks the merge

Write with the writer; read with the wasm reader *and* with the TypeScript/DuckDB path; diff the
three. Arrow demands two implementations and integration tests **before** a format change lands,
which is why its gaps are enumerated `skip` lines rather than wrong answers. GraphAr ships a shared
corpus with no round trip, and through that gap its fourth implementation landed having re-derived
the path arithmetic differently from the other three *and* from the corpus on disk — green CI on both
sides, because its tests asserted hand-written strings instead of resolving against the artefact.

## The format on that list we borrow from, and the one we do not conform to

GraphAr appears above as evidence, and it is also where this corpus gets its manifest vocabulary:
the three YAML filenames and all but three of the keys in them are GraphAr's. **That is a loan of
field names and not a claim of conformance**, and the boundary is stated once, with its divergences
enumerated and measured, in
[the corpus](/docs/design/corpus#what-is-borrowed-from-graphar-and-where-borrowing-stops). The rule
there is short enough to restate: *fossil follows GraphAr as far as GraphAr specifies, and no
further, because past that point there is nothing to follow but a C++ implementation.*

It belongs on this page because the worked example of "no further" is obligation 1's own pathology,
found in the format we borrow from. **GraphAr's specification does not define a count** — the word
appears zero times in `docs/specification/format.md` — and its reference implementation stores one
anyway, as `sizeof(int64_t)` raw bytes in the writing machine's byte order, in a file called
`vertex_count` that no specification names. No width, no endianness, no vector. That is below the
PMTiles line drawn above: PMTiles documents something badly, this documents nothing and is
normative by accident, which is exactly what the warning at the foot of this page describes with
Parquet's `created_by`.

Here the count is one of those three added keys — `vertex_count` and `edge_count`, decimal integers
in the manifest, required rather than optional, and checked against the rows on the disk by check 7
below. Which is the borrowing rule producing a *better* answer than the thing borrowed from, and it
only reads that way because the rule said in advance which of the two sources binds.

## What is true today

**The payload half of the fourth obligation exists**, and it is the half GraphAr specifically lacks.
It is what the citation in this page's frontmatter backs, and it is the only thing that citation
backs — obligations 1 and 2 have moved too, and what carries each of them is named beside it in
[the section that re-measures them](#what-is-absent-re-measured) rather than in a frontmatter field
that holds one path.

`crates/fossil-engine/tests/conformance.rs` — `the_corpus_keeps_the_promises_it_makes_to_a_stranger`
— compiles a small mapping, runs it, and then opens the produced tree with **plain SQL over a fresh
DuckDB connection**. It never touches `fossil-graph`, `fossil-df` or any other reader of ours. That
is the entire point: a test that reads back through the writer's own types proves the types are
self-consistent and nothing else. It cannot see a promise the format makes to somebody who is not us.

Ten numbered checks, most of them a count of violations, and the numbering is the test's own. This
table said six for as long as there were six; the tiling added three and `c573e73` added the counts
to the seventh:

| # | the promise |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | the corpus is not empty — the vacuity guard, and the reason it is numbered before the rest is in the callout below |
| 1 | `dense_id` is a gapless `0..n−1` with no repeats — a tile is a range of dense ids, so a gap is an addressable empty tile or an unaddressable vertex |
| 2 | each adjacency file is ordered by the key its name claims — `by_source` is CSR, `by_target` is CSC |
| 3 | the two orientations carry the same edge set; they are one relation stored twice and nothing else in the writer says so |
| 4 | every endpoint addresses a vertex that exists — the corruption renumbering could introduce and no row count would reveal |
| 5 | the run report agrees with what was written, because a corpus disagreeing with its own status is undetectable from either side alone. It used to say `RunStatus`, and the reason that type is gone is this invariant taken seriously: a description that can disagree with the bytes is one that eventually does, and `ac2a742` is the time it did |
| 6 | the staged single-file vertex Parquet is gone, not left behind as a stale second copy a reader would glob up |
| 7 | the manifest declares the tiling that was emitted, and **the counts it declares are the rows on disk** — the size is read off the artefact and every later assertion derives from it, so the test cannot agree with the writer by sharing a constant |
| 8 | every vertex is in the tile its own id names; a row in the wrong file is a vertex a reader will never fetch and never miss |
| 9 | the edges are tiled too, **both orientations**, each by the tile of the endpoint its file is ordered by — walked as a triple rather than written twice, because a check that only ever ran against `by_source` is how the target half went untiled |

It gates merges in the ordinary way: `cargo test --workspace` runs on every pull request.

<Callout title="Checked for vacuity, and mutation-tested">
Most assertions are a count of violations, so **an empty corpus satisfies them all at once** — which
is exactly how a conformance suite comes to pass vacuously and stops being evidence. That is what
check 0 is, and why it is numbered first rather than filed as setup; check 9 repeats it for the edge
directories, because an empty directory satisfies everything after it. Inverting the ordering
predicate reports 299 violations over 300 rows, which is how the ordering check is known to measure
the ordering rather than pass by construction.

Check 7's count half is the one with a stated limit on what it proves. The number is computed before
the layout post-pass, and the pass then re-reads, renumbers, re-tiles and deletes the file it was
given — it survives only because the pass writes one row out for every row it reads. So the count is
read back **off the tiles the pass wrote, after it ran**, rather than resting on the shape of that
code.
</Callout>

## What is absent, re-measured

Three bullets stood here: no vector published, no `BigInt` boundary enforced, no second reader. Two
of the three have moved, and the reason the first gave has been spent rather than answered — it said
the arithmetic was in motion because "the chunk is being retired for a 4,096-row tile", and the
4,096-row tile is what shipped. A tile is a fixed range of `dense_id` and its address is
`dense_id >> 12`.

**The vectors are published, and both sides execute them.** `apps/corpus/guards/vectors.json`
carries three tables — `tile_of`, seven borders including 2³¹ and 2⁵³; `morton2`, eleven including
bit 31, where an interleave that was not coerced back to unsigned sorts the top half of the plane
first; and `quantize`, five, with the width declared as part of the contract. The guard
`published-vectors` executes that file rather than restating it. `dd47c3b` put the Rust writer under
the same table for `quantize`, which until then had been a closure inside `morton_codes` and
therefore uncallable — the reference reader was pinned to the borders and the producer was free to
walk past them. Measured at the time: widen `quantize` to binary64 and the whole workspace stays
green, while `quantize(147, 0, 167)` moves 57687 → 57686 — one unit, which is a different Morton
code, rank, `dense_id` and tile.

<Callout title="Which is not all of the arithmetic">
No vector covers the bounding box `morton_codes` derives from the positions it is handed, and the
`::FLOAT` half of the contract — that DuckDB's `FLOAT / FLOAT` stays binary32 rather than promoting
— is evidenced by a passing guard rather than by a proof about widths. Both are named in
`crates/fossil-layout/src/layout.rs`'s own docblock, which is where a gap belongs when the test
beside it is the thing that cannot see it.
</Callout>

**`BigInt` at the boundary is enforced by a type, not by the lint rule that was decided.**
`packages/graph/src/address.ts` types `tileOf(denseId: bigint)` and every tile number as `bigint`,
and refuses a `Number` at runtime with a `TypeError` that names the reason. There is still no lint
rule, and today there is nowhere to write one: `lint` is `tsc --noEmit` in every package and the
repository contains no ESLint, Biome or oxlint configuration at all. So the guarantee holds exactly
as far as a signature reaches, and an id read out of a Parquet column into an untyped value is
outside it.

**One oracle holds for the verbs and was reversed by decision for the addressing.**
`@fossil-lang/graph`'s root barrel is the verb surface over `fossil-graph-wasm` — the same compiled
Rust, exactly as the third obligation asks. The addressing is the opposite on purpose:
`@fossil-lang/graph/address` is a TypeScript implementation with no WASM in its module graph, and
`packages/graph/tests/address-standalone.test.ts` compiles it into a package directory with no
`pkg/` and imports it from a child process to prove it. The reason is that a consumer without the
Rust toolchain could not load the package at all while the barrel was the only entry, and the price
is that the arithmetic now has two implementations — which is why the conformance table below is not
optional here the way it would be under a single oracle.

**There is a second reader, and the round trip it closes is over addresses rather than payload.**
`apps/corpus/conformance/reader.mjs` is the addressing in plain Node — no npm, no build — and the
published `resolveCorpus` is the other implementation, exercised by
`packages/graph/tests/conformance.test.ts`. Both execute `apps/corpus/conformance/expected.json`,
neither wrote it, and `.github/workflows/corpus.yml` runs the first on every pull request touching
`apps/corpus/**` or `packages/graph/**`. `writer.mjs` points that same reader at a tree `fossil run`
has just written, which is the only harness with the writer inside the loop; it needs a `fossil`
binary and is therefore not one of the CI steps.

<Callout title="And what obligation 4 still asks for">
**The wasm reader is in none of those loops**, so the three-way diff is a two-way one. And none of
them diffs *payload*: the address table says a file is where the manifest promised, not that its
columns hold what they claim. Payload is still one writer read by one independent engine — the
validator above — and `apps/corpus/guards/guards.mjs` records the residue in its own `cannotProve`:
*"a `dense_id` stored as a string opens, describes and addresses — until the reader shifts it."*
</Callout>

<Callout type="warn" title="Two things that would invalidate the destination, and one clock">
A **second writer** — anyone producing our corpus without being our Rust — inverts the priority: the
specification becomes the contract and the library becomes one implementation among others. A single
writer is what makes the library-shaped half safe. Two writers is what turned Parquet into what it
is, with a reference implementation normative by accident and `created_by` parsed against tables of
known-bad versions.

And the clock runs one way. **If the corpus reaches third parties before the vectors do, the option
is gone.** Parquet's problem is thirteen years of files that can no longer be withdrawn. Nothing is
published today, which is what makes this cheap exactly once.
</Callout>
