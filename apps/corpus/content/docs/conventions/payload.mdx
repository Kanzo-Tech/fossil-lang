The payload is **plain Parquet**. Files named for what they hold, columns named for what they are, no
specification in incubation between a reader and the bytes.

That is a choice with a discarded alternative behind it. The corpus borrows GraphAr's vocabulary —
CSR and CSC, dense ids, edges aligned to the source's chunk — and none of its wire format. As an
interchange format it does not pay: a recipient would have to learn a specification in incubation to
read `vertices.parquet`. Names that explain themselves are **more** interoperable, not less.

## Row groups of 4,096, and a tile is one

The addressing unit and the storage unit are the same number. A tile is 4,096 rows of `dense_id`;
a row group is 4,096 rows; a tile is a row group. Measured at five million vertices: 1,221 tiles,
1,221 row groups, no surprises in the remainder.

The default matters here more than it looks. A Parquet writer's default row-group size is often a
million rows or more, which puts five million vertices into **five** row groups and leaves the
footer with five boxes. **An addressed reader cannot prune with that.** Guard: `tile-of`, which
checks that a row group's ordinal is its address and that it never holds more rows than a tile.

## The footer is the index

Arithmetic tells a reader **which tiles exist**. It does not tell it **which tiles intersect the
window**, because the `dense_id` range of a rectangle is not computable without knowing the Morton
range, and the Morton range is what the boxes say.

The row-group statistics are those boxes, they are in the footer, and one request buys all of them.
At five million vertices in one file the footer is **496,373 bytes — 406 B per tile**; a reader
holds it once and computes byte ranges from then on without a second request. Contiguous tiles
coalesce for free: 22.3 selected tiles are 5.6 ranges.

<Callout type="warn" title="Read `min_value`/`max_value`, not `min`/`max`">
Parquet's original statistics fields were defined by **signed** byte comparison, which is meaningless
for an unsigned column. A writer that gets this right leaves them empty and fills `min_value` and
`max_value` instead — and `dense_id` is unsigned.

A reader that only knows the deprecated pair concludes that the footer carries no box for exactly
the column the whole address is built on. This cost the checker a false failure before it cost
anybody a corpus, which is the cheap way to find it.
</Callout>

Guard: `footer-is-the-index`, which asserts a box for `x`, `y` and `dense_id` on every vertex row
group and for `src_dense` on every edge tile. What it cannot prove is that the statistics are
**true**. They are written by the writer and believed by the reader; a box wider than its rows costs
a request, a box narrower than its rows loses vertices, and only re-reading every page tells them
apart.

### What is deliberately not in the footer

The Parquet **page index** — `ColumnIndex` and `OffsetIndex` — is written by some writers and not
others, and here it is 179 kB that no reader opens. It exists to prune *inside* a row group, and a
row group is 4,096 rows, which is the indivisible unit. There is nothing inside a tile to prune.

This is worth stating because the page index is the obvious reason to prefer one Parquet writer over
another, and it is the wrong one. What prunes is the row-group box, which every writer emits.

## Encoding: choose per column or lose a quarter of the bytes

Dictionary encoding is right for one of the four drawing columns and wrong for the others, and the
gap is large in both directions. Five million vertices, uncompressed pages:

| | `cluster_id` | `dense_id` | `x` | `y` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| dictionary everywhere | 71.6 kB | 27.56 MB | 27.50 MB | 20.00 MB |
| dictionary nowhere | 20.03 MB | 20.03 MB | 20.03 MB | 20.03 MB |
| **dictionary per column** | **71.6 kB** | **20.03 MB** | **20.03 MB** | 20.03 MB |

On `dense_id` and `x` — nearly unique within a tile — the dictionary plus its indices cost **37%
more than PLAIN**. On `cluster_id`, a handful of repeated values, it wins by **280×**. Turning it
off everywhere is worse than leaving it on everywhere (80.73 MB against 75.81 MB) because it
destroys `cluster_id`. Choosing per column lands at **60.78 MB**.

The corpus does not mandate an encoding — Parquet's whole point is that a reader does not care. It
mandates the consequence: a writer that applies one encoding decision to every column pays about a
quarter of its bytes, on disk and on the wire, for nothing.

## Geometry in the tile, everything else beside it

The drawing tile carries geometry. Measured on real tiles at five million: a tile of
`dense_id, x, y, community` costs **3 requests and 36.0 kB**; the same tile with all six columns
costs **4 requests and 37.0 kB**, from 68.8 kB stored. The same bytes on the wire, one round trip
fewer and half the storage.

Columns are laid out as separate arrays — `x` and `y`, never interleaved — which is what Parquet
stores anyway and what leaves the choice of layout to the reader.

<Callout type="warn" title="It does not remove the interleave, it moves it">
This paragraph claimed the layout lets a tile "upload to a GPU without transformation". That is
false for the GPU consumer this documentation cites as its own proof: cosmos.gl's
`setPointPositions` takes one interleaved `[x0, y0, x1, y1, …]` buffer and accepts nothing else, so
`kanzo-ui` interleaves on the way in. Separate columns are still right — a reader that wants them
apart gets them apart, and one that wants them together pays a single pass it would otherwise pay
twice — but the step is relocated to the reader rather than removed by construction.
</Callout>

## The payload format is open

**This is a design question, not a settled convention.**

A tile's payload is Parquet because the corpus is Parquet and one format is simpler than two. But a
tile is read to be drawn, and drawing wants contiguous typed arrays that upload to a GPU without
decoding. **Arrow IPC gives exactly that and Parquet does not** — Parquet's encodings are the reason
its bytes are small, and decoding them is work a drawing path pays on every tile.

What makes it a real question rather than a preference:

- The two formats disagree about *where* the cost goes. Parquet moves fewer bytes and decodes them;
  Arrow IPC moves more bytes and does not. Which wins depends on the link, and the link is the thing
  a corpus does not control.
- The addressing does not care. `tile = dense_id >> 12` is arithmetic over a range of rows, and it
  is true of a row group, of a file, and of an IPC record batch alike. **Nothing in
  [Addressing](/docs/conventions/addressing) or [Order](/docs/conventions/order) would change.**
- What would change is the footer. Parquet's row-group statistics are the index; an IPC file's
  metadata is not laid out to answer "which batches intersect this box", and replacing that is a
  design cost, not a swap.

This is also the strongest single argument against putting these conventions in a shared crate. **A
crate that models *the* manifest assumes one output format by construction**, and would have to be
rewritten to hold a question that the document and the guards hold without effort: the guards split
into the ones that are about addressing — which survive a format change untouched — and the ones
that are about Parquet, which are named as such.
