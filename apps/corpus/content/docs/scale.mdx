A corpus is written once and read many times, and the two halves meet the scale question on
completely different terms. **A read does not face it**, and the write side does: there, the
conventions here bound exactly one term, and the term they do not bound is the one that decides
whether a corpus can be larger than the machine that writes it.

## What a read costs does not follow the corpus

Every row below is measured on the page it belongs to, and the paragraph after the table is the
exception that has to be stated with them rather than after them.

| what a read costs | why it does not follow N | measured |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the address of a tile | [a tile is a range of `dense_id`](/docs/conventions/addressing), so the address is a shift | nothing is fetched to compute it and no directory is listed |
| the tiles a window needs | [the order](/docs/conventions/order) makes a rectangle a few contiguous runs of ids | at five million, a 20,000-vertex window is 179 runs covering 20,007 ids — 1.10×–1.20× over-read at tile granularity |
| a window | contiguous tiles coalesce into one byte range | 5.6 requests and 1.38 MB per window, on a corpus of 1,221 tiles |
| a hop | [two addressed reads](/docs/conventions/adjacency), never the relation | **113.0 kB at a quarter million and 113.0 kB at four million**, against 6.9 MB and 110.2 MB |

The hop row is the sharpest of the four: the addressed answer is the *same bytes* at sixteen times
the corpus, so the gap against reading the relation goes from 61× to 975× purely because the corpus
grew, and none of that growth is paid by the reader.

**One thing a reader holds does follow N, and it is worth stating rather than rounding away.** The
footer is [the index](/docs/conventions/payload), it arrives in one request, and it is **406 B per
tile** — 496,373 B for the 1,221 tiles of a five-million corpus. One box per 4,096 vertices is a
small constant, not a flat one. A reader's *queries* are flat in N; the index it holds to answer
them is N divided by the tile size, and a corpus large enough makes that a number rather than a
rounding error.

That is the whole of the read side's exposure. Everything else is on the write side.

## What the corpus contributes to the write side, and what it does not

The corpus's own ordering bounds the **edges** the pass that lays it out reads. That pass walks them
as the CSR they already are instead of rebuilding one, and the term went from **+2.09 GiB to
+0.08 GiB** at ten million vertices under a declared 4 GiB budget — the same change that took
**−1.71 GiB** off the process peak ([Adjacency](/docs/conventions/adjacency)).

It does not bound the **per-vertex state**. That is O(n) and resident — about **200 MB at ten
million** — and it is what the algorithm genuinely needs rather than a structure standing in for an
order the file already had. It is linear in the vertex count, and **no convention on this site makes
it otherwise.**

So the format's contribution to the write side is exactly one term, and the term left over is the
one a format cannot reach.

## The design question: larger than RAM

*Larger than RAM* is the claim on this site with the least evidence behind it.

A write is a job that declares how much memory it may use, and the operators inside it spill rather
than allocate past the declaration. That is a bound on a job. A corpus larger than the machine that
writes it is a different claim, and no measurement here separates the two: the largest corpus
measured is **ten million vertices**, and under a 4 GiB budget it peaks at **8.39 GiB** — under the
machine, not under the budget, and still growing with N.

The metric that decides the claim is the **peak heap** and not the wall clock. **What would settle it
is that curve staying flat across a size the machine cannot hold**, and no measurement here is that
one.

**That fossil could not write a larger-than-RAM corpus would be a finding as important as any
reading result**, and stating it that way is the point of writing it down here.

<Callout type="warn" title="Why a write path's own accounting is not on this site">
Which stage boundary holds a whole value, what a memory budget is as a protocol, which hypotheses
about a peak were killed by measurement — that is an accounting of a **writer**, not of the format.
It is a property of one implementation, and a second implementation that produced a conforming
corpus would owe none of it.

What belongs here is the part that survives changing the writer: what the conventions above bound,
what they do not, and the size at which the claim stops being demonstrated.
</Callout>
